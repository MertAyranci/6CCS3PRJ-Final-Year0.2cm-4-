\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Brief}
This chapter examines the literature on how the design of online messaging platforms shapes users’ ability to understand, manage, and revoke consent, with a specific focus on photo sharing on WhatsApp. It begins with a brief overview of digital messaging and photo-based communication to situate the social and privacy-sensitive nature of image sharing. It then reviews relevant theoretical and consent-related perspectives to explain why consent revocation is not only a technical issue but also a contextual and interpersonal one. The chapter subsequently examines research on instant messaging, photo sharing, and interface design to assess how current platform features support or constrain meaningful user control. Finally, it synthesises the literature to identify research gaps that motivate the proposed user-centred revocation design.


\section{Overview of Digital Messaging and Photo-Based Communication}
Digital messaging has developed from earlier forms of computer-mediated communication into mobile, app-based, and increasingly multimodal communication environments, where text, images, and other media are integrated within the same platforms. Herring’s historical overview highlights this shift from predominantly text-based CMC toward convergent and multimodal communication systems, providing a useful background for understanding contemporary messaging platforms \cite{Herring2019Coevolution}. Research on mobile messaging also shows how texting became embedded in everyday social contact, particularly in peer communication shaped by social norms and situational constraints \cite{ito_mobile_2005}. In parallel, early camera-phone studies show that images were used not only for personal capture but also for communication with absent others and remote tasks, helping establish photo messaging as a routine social practice \cite{kindberg_i_2005}. This transition is important because it created communication settings in which image sharing carries social meaning and privacy expectations that may later complicate consent revocation.


\section{Digital Privacy in Modern Communication}
This section grounds the dissertation in theories that explain why consent revocation is not simply a delete button, but a socially negotiated and context-dependent action.

\subsection{Communication Privacy Management (CPM)}
Communication Privacy Management (CPM) theory explains privacy as an ongoing process of boundary management rather than a fixed state, making it useful for analysing disclosure in interpersonal and mediated communication \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}. Petronio and Child (2020) describe CPM as a framework in which individuals treat private information as something they own and control, and they manage access to it through privacy rules shaped by context, relationship, perceived risk, and expectations about appropriate use after disclosure \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. A key concept is co-ownership: once information is shared, recipients may become legitimate co-owners who develop their own assumptions about how that information can be retained, discussed, or circulated \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. CPM therefore shifts attention from information itself to the negotiated rules and expectations that organise its flow. 

This matters for consent revocation in messaging because consent revocation in messaging is not only a technical question of whether content can be deleted; it is also a social question about whether previously established privacy expectations can be changed after disclosure \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}. CPM helps explain why post-sharing control is often contentious: a sender may understand revocation as a reassertion of personal control, while a recipient may interpret the same act as changing the rules after access has already been granted \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. Hence, CPM provides a strong theoretical foundation for studying consent and control in messaging-based photo sharing, where interpersonal expectations are central to how users judge fairness, trust, and appropriateness.

Applied to revocable or dynamic consent in messaging, CPM suggests that revocation should be understood as an attempt to re-coordinate privacy boundaries rather than simply erasing data \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. The theory’s concept of boundary turbulence is especially relevant here, as turbulence occurs when co-owners hold conflicting privacy rules or when expected information flows break down \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. In messaging environments such as WhatsApp-style photo sharing, this framework helps explain why consent may need to be updated over time and why platform mechanisms for revocation must operate within the tension between social boundary expectations and the technical ease of retention, copying, or redistribution \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}.


\subsection{Contextual Integrity (CI)}
Contextual Integrity (CI) reframes privacy as appropriateness of information flows, not simply secrecy or access control \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. Nissenbaum (2011) argues that information sharing is governed by contextual norms: who is involved (actors), what is shared (attributes), and how it moves (transmission principles) \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. This is directly relevant to WhatsApp photo sharing because users often treat a private chat as a bounded context with an implied “right audience,” even when the content is not technically protected from recipient-side redistribution. Under CI, harm occurs when a photo’s flow changes context (e.g., from a one-to-one chat into a group, another platform, or offline circulation) because the transfer violates the original contextual expectations, even if the sender originally shared willingly \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}.

CI is also useful for critiquing the limits of the notice-and-consent paradigm in everyday messaging. Nissenbaum (2011) critiques notice-and-consent as insufficient for online privacy governance, while Bourgeus and Vandercruysse (2024) argue that overreliance on consent often fails to produce meaningful privacy control in complex data ecosystems and motivate contextual and communal alternatives \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011,bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024}. This matters for photo sharing because images frequently involve group privacy: a photo can affect the sender, the recipient, and any depicted co-subjects, each with potentially different expectations about appropriate sharing \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024}. Design implication: revocation mechanisms should be framed as restoring context-appropriate flow (limiting further redistribution and future access within WhatsApp), and the UI should represent that permissions apply to a social context (audience and transmission rules), not just to an individual recipient or a one-time send action.

\subsection{Media Richness Theory (MRT) and Social Presence}

Media Richness Theory (MRT) holds that communication media vary in their capacity to convey information in ways that support interpretation and mutual understanding, and that richer media are generally better suited to tasks involving ambiguity (equivocality) \cite{daft_lengel_mrt_1986}. In Daft and Lengel’s account, rich media allow more immediate feedback and convey a greater variety of cues, which can help communicators clarify meaning and reduce misunderstanding when issues are socially sensitive or open to multiple interpretations \cite{daft_lengel_mrt_1986}. WhatsApp-style messaging that includes photo sharing can therefore be treated as relatively rich in everyday practice because it combines rapid interaction with visual content that can communicate relational and emotional information. At the same time, empirical work has qualified strong deterministic readings of MRT: Dennis and Kinney (1998) report that while cue and feedback properties shape perceptions of media richness, matching media richness to task equivocality did not reliably improve performance outcomes in their study \cite{dennis_kinney_1998}.

Social Presence Theory provides a complementary lens by emphasising the interpersonal qualities of mediated communication. Short, Williams, and Christie (1976) conceptualise social presence as the extent to which a medium conveys the salience of the interaction partner and the sense of interpersonal involvement, with higher social presence generally associated with greater warmth and immediacy in interaction \cite{short_williams_christie_1976}. In contemporary messaging contexts, this helps explain why users may prefer channels that support richer interpersonal signalling for relational communication, even when such channels also create additional privacy and control demands. Ishii et al. (2019) further argue that media richness should be revisited for modern technologies because users’ experiences of richness are shaped not only by channel properties but also by evolving features and patterns of use, which is particularly relevant for mobile messaging environments \cite{ishii_etal_2019}.

Taken together, MRT and Social Presence Theory help explain why revocable or dynamic consent in messaging is not only a technical problem but also a socially consequential one. When users select richer media for intimate or socially sensitive exchange, the interaction may carry stronger interpersonal involvement and relational expectations about how shared content ought to be handled \cite{daft_lengel_mrt_1986,short_williams_christie_1976}. In such contexts, withdrawing consent may be experienced as a delicate change in interpersonal boundaries rather than as a neutral technical action, because it can signal mistrust or create a sense of relational distance. Design implication: revocation mechanisms in WhatsApp-style photo sharing should be designed with attention to this social meaning, supporting face-saving and relationship maintenance while clearly communicating the practical limits of technical control in the presence of retention and recirculation practices \cite{dennis_kinney_1998,ishii_etal_2019}.

\section{The Concept of Consent}
This section examines consent in digital communication and argues that, in messaging contexts, it should be understood as an ongoing process rather than a one-time decision. While legal frameworks define the formal conditions under which consent is considered valid, consent in interpersonal messaging also functions as a relational and contextual expectation about what others may appropriately do with shared content.

\subsection{The Legal Mandate}
Although the GDPR is not designed specifically for consent revocation in online messaging platforms, it provides a useful benchmark for how valid consent and withdrawal should be implemented in such environments. Under the GDPR, consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, and withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent; where consent is withdrawn and no other legal basis remains, the right to erasure may also be engaged \cite{gdpr_2016,politou_2018}. Chaurasiya (2025) shows that the Indian DPDP Act adopts a similar consent-centred approach by defining consent in affirmative terms and recognising mechanisms for managing and withdrawing it \cite{chaurasiya_consent_2025}. However, as Politou et al. (2018) note, revocation operates prospectively rather than retroactively, and erasure becomes technically difficult once data have been copied, shared, or replicated across systems \cite{politou_2018}. In distributed communication contexts such as messaging, the legal mandate therefore establishes a right to revoke consent, but not a guarantee of complete practical forgetting.

\subsection{Consent Fatigue, the Validity Gap, and Non-Use}
Although legal and policy frameworks often treat consent as meaningful once it has been formally obtained, this assumption is difficult to sustain in contemporary digital environments. Baruh and Popescu argue that privacy self-management based on notice and choice places too much responsibility on individuals, even though data practices are often opaque, complex, and difficult to resist in practice \cite{baruh_big_2017}. In such settings, consent may be legally valid without being substantively meaningful, creating a validity gap between formal agreement and real user control. They further show that users may respond in two limited ways: through partial withdrawal or avoidance when privacy risks feel unmanageable, or through assimilation, where users accept reduced control and rely on market-provided protections \cite{baruh_big_2017}.

Waldman extends this critique by showing that privacy is often operationalised narrowly within organisations. In his account, lawyers tend to reduce privacy to notice and choice, while engineers often equate it with security or encryption, leaving limited space for more user-centred understandings of consent and control \cite{waldman_designing_nodate}. For the present topic, this suggests that effective revocation cannot depend only on formal consent or interface disclosure, but must be supported by designs that make control realistic and usable in practice.

\subsection{Consent Models}

Consent in everyday messaging cannot be understood adequately as a one-off, individualised act of permission, because interpersonal communication unfolds over time, across relationships, and under conditions of partial and often irreversible loss of control. Most of the revised consent models discussed below were developed in health, research, or broader data-governance settings rather than instant messaging; they are used here analytically, as conceptual tools for thinking about permission, revision, and withdrawal in WhatsApp-style communication. Traditional consent frameworks tend to assume that consent is static, singular, and transaction-like, yet this logic becomes unstable in ongoing interpersonal exchange, where the meaning of permission can shift after disclosure. As Burkhardt et al. suggest, meaningful consent depends not only on formal agreement but also on understanding and perceived control, while work on consent and revocation has also shown that decisions made at one moment cannot remain fully informed across the lifetime of data use \cite{burkhardt_privacy_2023,agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012}. This is why dynamic consent has been positioned as a response to one-off authorisation models that ask people to agree to future uses they may neither anticipate nor revisit \cite{schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022}. In messaging, these weaknesses are particularly visible because permission is shaped by repeated interaction, changing context, recipient interpretation, and what happens after content has already been sent.

Dynamic Consent offers the clearest correction to this temporal problem by treating permission as revisable rather than fixed. Across the literature, it is characterised by ongoing engagement, granular choice, and the possibility of adjusting preferences over time rather than accepting a single all-or-nothing decision \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,lee_identifying_2023}. This broader orientation is reinforced by Wilbanks’ argument that digital consent should be designed as the beginning of an ongoing ethical relationship rather than a one-time administrative step, and by later work that frames modifiable consent choices as central to user-centred data governance \cite{wilbanks_design_2018,narkhede_enhancing_2025}. However, what Dynamic Consent improves most clearly is temporality, not post-disclosure control. In everyday messaging, the ability to revise a preference does not necessarily translate into the ability to affect already-delivered content once it has moved beyond the sender’s immediate control.

Revocable Consent is therefore closer to the central concern of this dissertation, because it foregrounds withdrawal after permission has already been granted. The literature usefully distinguishes between revocability as a normative requirement and revocation as a practically enforceable outcome. Consent and revocation have been framed as ongoing controls, with informed revocation emphasising that users should understand the forms of withdrawal available to them \cite{agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012}. Related technical work similarly treats withdrawal as a change in the scope of authorised access: users should be able to specify who may access data, for what purposes, and for how long, while formal models of consent evolution represent revocation by closing a previously open interval of collection or access rather than undoing prior disclosure \cite{merlec_smart_2021,robol_consent_2023}. This distinction is especially important for photo sharing, where a sender may be able to end future authorised access without being able to retract a photo that has already been viewed, saved, or copied by a recipient. Revocation in messaging is therefore best understood as limiting future legitimacy and platform-supported access, not as guaranteeing recovery of the original disclosure. This problem is reinforced by the broader notice-and-consent literature, which shows that even ostensibly reversible interfaces can fail to produce meaningful control when the design of choice itself is manipulative or unclear \cite{nouwens_dark_2020}.

Other models address narrower weaknesses in static consent without resolving the broader problem. Retroactive consent is useful because it makes the timing of authorisation explicit, allowing consent and withdrawal to be analysed in terms of whether their effects apply to past or future data practices \cite{robol_consent_2023}. A related response is to reduce persistence prospectively by limiting the duration or openness of permission. Although Im et al. do not formalise ephemeral consent as a standalone governance model, their emphasis on consent as revertible and specific supports the idea that permission should be bounded and revisable rather than indefinite \cite{im_yes_2021}. Yet these approaches remain partial. Once content has been copied, remembered, or redistributed by others, neither temporal expiry nor after-the-fact adjustment can fully restore sender control.

Seen comparatively, these models should not be treated as mutually exclusive competitors, but as attempts to repair different weaknesses in conventional consent. Dynamic Consent responds most clearly to temporality; Revocable Consent foregrounds withdrawal and reversibility; retroactive approaches sharpen how timing affects authorisation; and ephemeral approaches seek to limit persistence before problems arise. What unites them is a shared difficulty in accounting for messaging as a social practice in which the meaning and consequences of disclosure continue to evolve after the moment of sending \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,robol_consent_2023,politou_2018,im_yes_2021}. The central problem is therefore not simply lack of choice, but the challenge of aligning revisability, revocability, relationality, and downstream loss of control within a single model.

For this reason, affirmative and ongoing consent offers the strongest conceptual direction for this dissertation. Im et al. describe affirmative consent as voluntary, informed, revertible, specific, and unburdensome, providing a framework that treats permission as active and revisable rather than presumed or exhausted at a single point in time \cite{im_yes_2021}. These principles fit messaging more closely than one-off models because they better reflect how consent is negotiated in unfolding interaction. At the same time, they should not be overstated as a technical solution to screenshots, retention, or redistribution. Their value is conceptual and normative: they offer a clearer account of what responsible design should support, namely permission that can be revisited, clarified, limited, and renegotiated without assuming that post-sharing control can ever be complete.

This issue becomes even more complex in photo sharing, where consent may extend beyond a single sender-recipient relationship. 

Taken together, the literature suggests that the main weakness of conventional consent models is not simply insufficient granularity, but their inability to account for the ongoing social life of shared content. Improved models repair important dimensions of this problem, but they still encounter hard limits once messages and photos enter interpersonal networks where recipients can retain, reinterpret, copy, or redistribute them. For everyday messaging, meaningful consent is therefore best understood not as a completed transaction but as an ongoing and socially situated process whose practical force depends on both relational norms and the technical limits of post-sharing control.


\section{Consent in Instant Messaging and Photo Sharing}
This section connects the consent concepts and theories to the specific realities of photo sharing in instant messaging, focusing on WhatsApp context.

\subsection{Multi-Party Privacy Conflicts (MPCs)}

Multi-party privacy conflicts arise when a single photo implicates several people whose privacy interests do not align, making individualised models of consent inadequate for photo sharing. In the photo-sharing literature, MPCs are generally understood as situations in which the person who decides to share content is not the only person who bears the privacy consequences of that disclosure. One user’s act of sharing can expose others depicted in the image, even where those others have had no meaningful opportunity to approve, refuse, or shape that disclosure. Cherubini et al. show that this problem is especially salient for co-owned content such as group photos, while Liu et al.’s survey of image privacy similarly suggests that privacy in online image sharing is shaped not only by image content itself but also by relationships, context, and multi-party interaction \cite{cherubini_2021, liu_privacy_2023}. Applied to instant messaging, this means that a shared photo may simultaneously involve the sender, the recipient, and any depicted co-subjects, each of whom may hold different expectations about whether the image should be sent, retained, forwarded, or later withdrawn. Consent in photo sharing is therefore not only ongoing, as argued in the previous subsection, but also distributed across multiple stakeholders from the outset, which makes sender-only models of permission normatively and practically incomplete \cite{cherubini_2021, liu_privacy_2023}.

The literature proposes several responses to multi-party privacy conflicts, but these approaches often manage the problem more effectively than they resolve it. Cherubini et al. distinguish between precautionary mechanisms, which prevent or constrain sharing before disclosure, and dissuasion mechanisms, which seek to discourage risky sharing by prompting reflection on possible consequences \cite{cherubini_2021}. They find that participants generally prefer precautionary mechanisms because these provide stronger protection for affected parties and reduce uncertainty for uploaders, although they also introduce friction and depend on identifying co-owners in advance \cite{cherubini_2021}. This limitation is reflected in technical proposals. Xu et al. (2017) propose a system in which people appearing in a photo can be identified and involved in the sharing decision, but this depends on a privacy-preserving facial-recognition process to detect co-owners in the first place \cite{xu_my_2017}. Again in another paper, Xu et al. (2019), similarly develop a trust-based privacy-preserving sharing model that allows selective protection of co-owned content, yet the approach still treats the problem largely through pre-sharing optimisation and does not fully incorporate direct negotiation among all affected parties \cite{xu_trust-based_2019}. Zhang et al. (2023) extend this critique by arguing that privacy-preserving frameworks often define ownership too narrowly and do not fully address the risks introduced by subsequent forwarding and cross-platform dissemination \cite{zhang_go-sharing_2023}. Taken together, these approaches are important because they show that co-ownership cannot be reduced to sender-side privacy settings alone, but they remain largely pre-sharing, system-centred, or prediction-based, and therefore struggle to reconcile competing interests once the image becomes socially and technically mobile \cite{cherubini_2021, xu_my_2017, xu_trust-based_2019, zhang_go-sharing_2023}.

Multi-party privacy conflicts therefore show that consent revocation in messaging is not simply a matter of allowing one sender to change their mind, but a broader problem of shared stakes and weakening control once images begin to circulate \cite{cherubini_2021, zhang_go-sharing_2023}.

\subsection{Persistence, Recipient Control, and the Limits of Revocation}

Multi-party privacy conflicts show that control over a photo may already be shared before it is sent; the next limit is structural. In instant messaging, revocation does not operate as ongoing access control after delivery, because the system’s basic function is to transmit content to a recipient-controlled device rather than to preserve sender authority over it indefinitely. For that reason, revocation is best understood as prospective and partial: once a photo has been delivered, a platform may still alter later in-app availability, but it cannot guarantee retrieval of the original disclosure. This is consistent with earlier work on revocation as a forward-looking rather than fully retroactive mechanism, and with Schnitzler et al.’s observation that deleting messages from a recipient’s history is substantially harder than deleting them locally, even in closed messaging ecosystems \cite{agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012, politou_2018, schnitzler_2020}.

WhatsApp provides a useful case because its privacy architecture and its revocation features point in different directions. On the one hand, WhatsApp emphasises end-to-end encryption and states in its UK Privacy Policy that messages are typically stored on users’ devices rather than on its servers once delivery has occurred; on the other hand, this very model means that post-delivery control is necessarily limited \cite{whatsapp_e2e_encryption, whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. Deletion, disappearing messages, and view-once media should therefore be interpreted as bounded platform-level controls over later visibility, not as guarantees of erasure. WhatsApp’s own help documentation states that recipients may see a message before deletion succeeds, that unsuccessful deletion may not be flagged to the sender, and that iOS recipients may still have media saved in Photos even after the message is deleted from the chat \cite{whatsapp_delete_messages}. Likewise, disappearing messages only affect new messages and can be kept in the chat, while unopened view-once media can still be restored from backups; even newer protections such as Advanced Chat Privacy are described as helping prevent content from being taken outside WhatsApp by restricting export and auto-download, which reinforces that these features mitigate persistence routes rather than eliminate them \cite{whatsapp_disappearing_messages, whatsapp_view_once, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy_blog_2025}.

The weakness of revocation is not only technical but also interpretive. Schnitzler et al. show that users do not always correctly understand what a deletion action actually does, and that clearer interface explanations improve their ability to assess deletion outcomes; in their study, participants in the WhatsApp condition understood message deletion more accurately than those in the Skype condition, partly because WhatsApp more clearly distinguishes local from broader deletion effects \cite{schnitzler_2020}. The same study also shows that deletion notices and other residual traces matter socially: participants were sometimes surprised not by whether deletion occurred, but by the notification that a message had been deleted, and some explicitly associated such traces with mistrust or ambiguity. In this sense, revocation in WhatsApp photo sharing is also an expectation-management problem. If interface cues suggest stronger control than the system can actually deliver, users may overestimate what deletion has achieved; if revocation is made too visibly traceable, it may also generate interpersonal friction rather than simply restore privacy \cite{schnitzler_2020}.

If this subsection shows that revocation is already limited within the platform once a photo has been delivered, the next subsection shows that persistence becomes even harder to govern when recipients can capture and recirculate content through channels the platform cannot fully control.

\subsection{The Screenshot Threat}

The screenshot threat matters because it creates a form of persistence that no longer depends primarily on the platform's own retention settings. WhatsApp's privacy-oriented features, including disappearing messages, `view once' media, and advanced chat privacy, can limit some later visibility or sharing within the app, but they do not fully govern what a recipient can do after viewing content \cite{whatsapp_disappearing_messages, whatsapp_view_once, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy, whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. As Heath et al.\ note, even disappearing-message systems leave open preservation routes such as forwarding, screenshotting, copying, saving, or photographing content before it disappears \cite{heath_2023}. As Ingber argues, screenshots turn part of a conversation into a new file on the recipient's device, making it easier to store, reuse, and circulate beyond the original exchange \cite{ingber_2025}. Screenshotting therefore creates a parallel persistence route that sits partly outside the logic of in-app revocation and ephemerality: even if the original message becomes less visible within WhatsApp, a copied representation may continue to exist elsewhere. More broadly, image-privacy scholarship identifies the recipient side as a continuing source of privacy risk once content is already in circulation, because recipients may retain or recirculate images beyond the sender's intended audience \cite{liu_privacy_2023}.

What makes screenshots especially important for this dissertation is that they are not only technical workarounds but socially meaningful acts. Ingber distinguishes screenshot collection from screenshot sharing and shows that both can generate distinct privacy harms, because they move co-owned information into a new context without notice or consent \cite{ingber_2025}. This matters for consent because, as Im et al.\ note, affirmative consent must be specific: permission to share a photo with one recipient does not automatically extend to copying or recirculating it beyond the original interaction \cite{im_yes_2021}. In WhatsApp photo sharing, screenshots therefore make revocation not only technically weak but normatively contested, as recipients may treat capture as harmless remembering, evidence, or legitimate reuse while senders may understand it as a breach of containment and trust.

For this reason, screenshot risk should be treated not as a marginal loophole but as a central challenge for any interface that presents revocation or ephemerality as meaningful user control \cite{ingber_2025, heath_2023, liu_privacy_2023}.

\section{Human Computer Interaction (HCI) and Interface Design}
This section synthesises HCI research on why consent and revocation controls in digital platforms often fail in practice, and which interaction patterns can support meaningful, revisable user control in fast-paced messaging photo-sharing contexts \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024,wilbanks_design_2018}. It focuses on the usability and integrity of control—whether users can locate, understand, and confidently act on privacy options without being nudged or steered toward platform-favoured outcomes \cite{nouwens_dark_2020}.

\subsection{Interface Failures and Manipulative Patterns}
Even when platforms formally provide privacy and consent controls, HCI research shows these mechanisms often fail to produce meaningful user control at the interface level. This is an issue that becomes especially consequential in messaging-based photo sharing, where disclosures are often intimate, fast, and difficult to reverse once an image leaves the sender’s device. Classic notice-and-choice approaches are frequently ineffective because notices are not usable or useful in practice, are often ignored, and are frequently treated as something bolted onto a system rather than integrated into its interaction design \cite{schaub_notice_2015}. This breakdown is reinforced by broader critiques that modern data ecosystems create information asymmetries and complexity that make privacy self-management unrealistic: privacy policies are often too long and complex to support real understanding, resulting in passive acceptance rather than informed decision-making \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024,wilbanks_design_2018}. In messaging and social media settings, this becomes visible as concrete usability failures: expert evaluations using privacy-focused heuristics find that the ability to revoke consent is among the most problematic areas across popular apps, and that privacy information is inconsistently placed in menus in ways that undermine core usability principles such as consistency \cite{albesher_2021}. For photo sharing, the implication is straightforward: users cannot withdraw consent meaningfully if image-related controls (e.g., deletion, visibility, sharing restrictions) are hard to locate, hard to compare, and ambiguous about what they actually change—particularly when users are acting quickly in-chat and assume a “delete” action restores privacy.

This problem is intensified when interface designs do not merely confuse users but actively steer them toward platform-preferred outcomes. Empirical work on consent dialogs shows that “consent” interfaces commonly use design tactics that encourage acceptance of defaults (e.g., making ``accept'' more prominent, hiding configuration behind additional steps, or omitting an obvious decline path), so the banner functions more like a notification than a genuine control mechanism \cite{bermejo_fernandez_2021}. Analyses of GDPR-oriented consent pop-ups likewise argue that dark-pattern strategies can be used to circumvent the regulation’s intent ``by design,'' and that rights-protecting regulation is weakened if interface design is left unaddressed \cite{soe_2020}. From a legal/HCI perspective, this steering is especially problematic because valid consent requires a meaningful choice and the ability to withdraw consent, and designs that make ``accept'' more salient than ``reject'' or that hide privacy-relevant options undermine that balance \cite{gray_etal_2021}. Experimental evidence also suggests that cookie-consent regimes often do not work as intended: participants frequently select privacy-unfriendly options and report low perceived control, raising concerns that interface-level nudges and defaults can hollow out the ideal of deliberative consent \cite{grasl_dark}. For WhatsApp-style photo sharing, the key implication is that revocation controls must not only exist but must be reachable, interpretable, and resistant to interface choices that overstate user agency or make withdrawal harder than acceptance.

Taken together, these failures motivate the next subsection’s focus on what interaction patterns can actually support informed, revisable consent---for example, by treating notice timing and control presentation as explicit design dimensions \cite{schaub_notice_2015} and by using interaction techniques that increase user attention to what they are disclosing \cite{lindegren_evaluation_2021}.

\subsection{Interactive Design for Meaningful and Revocable Consent}
Constructive HCI work suggests that meaningful consent is best supported when it is designed as an explicit, understandable interaction rather than a frictionless checkbox event. In e-consent, Wilbanks argues that interface design shapes whether people actually attend to and understand what they are agreeing to, implying that consent should be treated as a designed experience that encourages comprehension rather than mere completion \cite{wilbanks_design_2018}. Relatedly, affirmative-consent work frames consent as requiring conditions such as being informed, specific, and revertible, which translates at the interface level into making actions intentional, consequences legible, and outcomes visible \cite{im_yes_2021}. On mobile devices in particular, interaction techniques can be used to increase user engagement with the consent act itself: Lindegren et al.\ show that alternative smartphone consent designs intended to engage users (beyond a standard tap-through) can increase attention to what is being agreed to \cite{lindegren_evaluation_2021}. For messaging photo sharing, these insights imply that consent-relevant controls should be embedded into the moment of sending in a way users can notice under time pressure, and should provide clear feedback and states so users can tell what has changed after they act.

The same interactional logic extends from initial permission to later revision and withdrawal. Schaub et al.\ emphasise that the effectiveness of privacy notices depends on design dimensions such as timing and delivery context, supporting the use of just-in-time and contextually placed cues rather than relying on static policies \cite{schaub_notice_2015}. For photo sharing, this points toward revocation controls that remain reachable after sending and are paired with explanations that honestly communicate scope. Where revocation is difficult to operationalise after disclosure, HCI work on multiparty privacy conflicts shows that design can intervene earlier through precautionary mechanisms (e.g., requiring collaboration/approval) or dissuasive mechanisms (e.g., friction or warnings that prompt reconsideration) \cite{cherubini_2021}; these approaches are especially relevant to photo sharing because they can reduce avoidable disclosures before images become socially and technically mobile. Dynamic-consent literature strengthens this orientation by treating permissions as reviewable and updateable over time via interface-supported communication and preference management \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022,lee_identifying_2023}. Technical proposals for dynamic consent management further highlight capabilities such as explicit expression of consent choices, access to consent history, and withdrawal support across a data lifecycle \cite{merlec_smart_2021}, while adoption-focused reviews emphasise that continuous communication and multiple modalities can facilitate use, but also note practical barriers such as consent fatigue and implementation complexity \cite{lee_identifying_2023}. For WhatsApp-style photo sharing, the design takeaway is not to promise perfect post-sharing control, but to build interactions that help users act deliberately at sharing time, understand the practical scope of revocation, and revisit decisions later through clear, contextual, and interpretable controls.

Overall, for messaging-based photo sharing the central HCI challenge is to design consent and revocation interactions that remain understandable and revisable over time, without implying that interface controls can fully overcome the limits of downstream retention and redistribution.



\section{Empirical Usability Assessments of Current Platforms}
Empirical evaluations suggest that revocation-related privacy controls are frequently available in principle but weak in usable effectiveness. In a STRAP-based expert heuristic evaluation of WhatsApp, Twitter, and Snapchat, Albesher and Alhussain identify the ``ability to revoke consent'' as the most notable problem area across all three apps, noting that none provides a clear way to revoke the underlying user agreement and that revocation (where offered) can be conditional or unreliable in its practical effect \cite{albesher_2021}. They also report that privacy information and settings are presented inconsistently across apps, undermining basic usability principles such as consistency and making control harder to locate and interpret \cite{albesher_2021}. Complementary legal-empirical evidence from the web consent ecosystem reinforces this availability--effectiveness gap: Kancherla et al.\ find that many popular sites make revocation interfaces absent or more complex than acceptance, and that revocation is often not correctly recorded or communicated to third parties, with tracking sometimes persisting even after users attempt to withdraw consent \cite{kancherla_2025}. Taken together, these results support a general empirical takeaway: revocation tends to be a high-severity usability weakness, not simply a missing feature.

WhatsApp provides a concrete case of revocation-adjacent controls that remain bounded and context-dependent rather than fully reliable or granular consent management. Platform features such as message deletion, disappearing messages, view-once media, and chat-level protections can reduce some forms of persistence and re-sharing within the app, but they operate as mitigations rather than guaranteed erasure or downstream control \cite{whatsapp_delete_messages,whatsapp_disappearing_messages,whatsapp_view_once,whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy,whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. Forensic analysis of ephemeral messaging further illustrates why these controls remain partial in practice: Heath et al.\ document how disappearing-message behaviour varies across platforms and storage locations, and they note WhatsApp’s own acknowledgement that content can be preserved through actions such as replying (quoted content), forwarding into contexts where disappearing settings differ, or capturing content via screenshots/photography before expiry \cite{heath_2023}. Their results also show that message timers and residual artefacts can remain in application databases, reinforcing that ephemerality does not automatically eliminate persistence \cite{heath_2023}. Overall, existing platforms provide controls that gesture toward revocation, but empirical and platform evidence suggests these controls remain partial, inconsistently interpretable, and weaker in practice than their presence implies.

\section{Research Gap and Summary}
Taken together, the literature reviewed in this chapter shows that consent revocation in messaging is neither a purely technical function nor a fully solved interface problem. Earlier sections establish that revocation is socially negotiated, only partially effective after delivery, vulnerable to forwarding and screenshots, and difficult to translate into interaction models that users can understand and act on in practice \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,politou_2018,heath_2023,ingber_2025,wilbanks_design_2018,nouwens_dark_2020}. The remaining research gaps therefore concern not whether revocation matters, but how it can be operationalised, scoped, and evaluated in WhatsApp-like photo-sharing contexts.

\subsection{RG1: Translating dynamic/ongoing consent into usable messaging UI}
The chapter has shown that dynamic, ongoing, and revocable consent are conceptually better suited to messaging than one-off authorisation, because they treat permission as revisable over time rather than exhausted at the moment of sending \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022,lee_identifying_2023,im_yes_2021}. However, the literature reviewed here provides much stronger conceptual and governance-level accounts than concrete guidance on how such models become low-friction, intelligible interaction flows within routine messaging. The unresolved issue is therefore one of translation: how ongoing consent can be expressed through usable controls, reviewable states, and in-context permission management for everyday photo sharing without overwhelming users or overstating what those controls can achieve \cite{merlec_smart_2021,wilbanks_design_2018}.

\subsection{RG2: Post-delivery control is partial and easily misunderstood under real redistribution conditions}
The chapter also shows that real platforms already offer deletion and ephemerality features, but that these operate only as bounded post-delivery mitigations rather than enforceable recall. In WhatsApp-like systems, revocation becomes structurally weaker once content reaches recipient-controlled devices, and users do not always interpret deletion cues or feature scope accurately \cite{politou_2018,schnitzler_2020,whatsapp_delete_messages,whatsapp_disappearing_messages,whatsapp_view_once}. This problem intensifies under actual redistribution conditions, because forwarding, saving, screenshotting, and other capture practices create persistence routes beyond platform-scoped revocation \cite{heath_2023,ingber_2025}. The remaining gap is therefore not simply that current controls are imperfect, but that there is still no robust model for post-delivery control that remains user-comprehensible once circulation extends beyond the original in-app exchange.

\subsection{RG3: Multi-party privacy and fairness are under-supported in revocation interaction design}
Across the chapter, privacy has been framed as relational, context-dependent, and frequently multi-party: once a photo is shared, senders, recipients, and depicted co-subjects can all have legitimate but competing stakes in how it is retained, circulated, or withdrawn \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,nissenbaum_contextual_2011,cherubini_2021,liu_privacy_2023}. Yet current messaging tools largely encode revocation as a sender-centred action, offering little support for co-subject agency, negotiation, or explanations that make revocation intelligible to all affected parties. The unresolved issue is therefore one of fairness as well as control: the literature does not yet offer a clear interaction model for revocation that balances stakeholder visibility, minimises unnecessary interpersonal blame, and remains understandable in socially sensitive exchanges \cite{cherubini_2021,ingber_2025}.

\subsection{RG4: Limited WhatsApp-specific empirical understanding of revocable photo-permission expectations}
The reviewed literature gives a strong account of why WhatsApp is an important case: its controls are widely used, bounded in scope, and socially meaningful in ways that affect trust, interpretation, and downstream sharing \cite{whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025,whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy,whatsapp_delete_messages,schnitzler_2020,ingber_2025}. It also provides platform documentation, heuristic usability evaluation, and evidence about screenshot or deletion effects \cite{albesher_2021,schnitzler_2020,heath_2023,ingber_2025}. What remains under-evidenced, however, is what WhatsApp users themselves expect from revocable photo permissions in ordinary relational contexts: what forms of control they would understand, trust, accept, or regard as socially fair. The final gap is therefore empirical and context-specific, centring on user expectations rather than on the technical existence of platform features.

\subsection{Summary}
Together, these gaps show that the central problem is not simply whether revocation exists, but whether it can be translated into interaction models that are understandable, fair across stakeholders, realistic about post-delivery limits, and grounded in the expectations of WhatsApp users. They therefore motivate the need for a messaging-appropriate mechanism that supports usable, socially intelligible, and realistically scoped revocation of photo access in WhatsApp-like contexts.
