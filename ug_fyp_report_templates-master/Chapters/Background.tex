
\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Brief}
This chapter reviews how online messaging platform design affects users’ ability to manage consent in photo sharing, with a focus on WhatsApp. It outlines theories of privacy and consent, reviews platform features and interface research, and identifies the research gaps that justify this project.

\section{Digital Messaging and Photo Sharing} 
Digital messaging has shifted from text-based CMC toward multimodal environments where images are routine \cite{Herring2019Coevolution}. Early mobile and camera-phone studies show that photo messaging became embedded in everyday social practice, carrying privacy expectations that complicate later consent revocation \cite{kindberg_i_2005}.

\section{Digital Privacy in Modern Communication}

\subsection{Communication Privacy Management (CPM)}

Communication Privacy Management (CPM) theory treats privacy as ongoing boundary management rather than a fixed state, in which individuals own and control private information through rules shaped by context and post-disclosure expectations \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}. A key concept is co-ownership which emphasises, once information is shared, recipients may become legitimate co-owners who develop their own assumptions about how that information can be used or circulated \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. 

For consent revocation in messaging, CPM highlights a core tension. A sender may understand revocation as reasserting personal control, while a recipient may see it as changing the rules after access has already been granted \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. This helps explain why post-sharing control is often contentious in messaging environments, where interpersonal expectations shape how users judge trustworthiness, and appropriateness \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}. These tensions are more evident in photo sharing because disclosure is typically associated with continuing relationships. As a result, attempts to withdraw access may be interpreted as relational signals such as mistrust, disappointment, or a reconsideration of boundaries, in addition to privacy management. 

CPM’s concept of boundary turbulence is relevant here. Turbulence occurs when co-owners hold conflicting privacy rules or when expected information flows break down \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. In messaging environments such as WhatsApp-style photo sharing, this helps explain why consent may need to be revisited over time and why revocation mechanisms must operate within the tension between social boundary expectations and the technical ease of saving, or redistribution \cite{child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011, petronio_cpm_2020}.

\subsection{Contextual Integrity (CI)}
Contextual Integrity (CI) reframes privacy as appropriateness of information flows, not simply secrecy or access control \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. Nissenbaum (2011) argues that information sharing is governed by contextual norms: who is involved (actors), what is shared (attributes), and how it moves (transmission principles) \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. For WhatsApp photo sharing, this matters because users often treat a private chat as a bounded context with an implied “right audience,” even though recipients may still redistribute the content. Under CI, harm occurs when a photo’s flow changes context (e.g., from a one-to-one chat into a group, another platform, or offline circulation) because the transfer violates the original contextual expectations, even if the sender originally shared willingly \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}.
 
CI is also useful for critiquing the limits of the notice-and-consent paradigm in everyday messaging. Nissenbaum (2011), argues that notice-and-consent does not provide adequate online privacy governance \cite{nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. Likewise, relying too heavily on consent typically results in little or no real privacy control in large-scale data environments \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024}. This is important for photo sharing because images frequently involve group privacy. A photo can affect the sender, the recipient, and any depicted co-subjects, each with potentially different expectations about appropriate sharing \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024}. In a scenario where photos are being shared via WhatsApp style means, CI frames revocation as an attempt to restore context-appropriate information flow by governing future access and redistribution.

\subsection{Media Richness Theory (MRT) and Social Presence}

Media Richness Theory (MRT) states that rich media can provide better interpretations of meaning than leaner media, especially when messages contain ambiguity or sensitivity. \cite{daft_lengel_mrt_1986}. Furthermore, Social Presence Theory explains how media makes another person feel psychologically present and immediate \cite{short_williams_christie_1976}. Ishii et al.\ argue that these experiences are shaped not only by channel features but also by evolving social patterns of use \cite{ishii_etal_2019}. Both frameworks are relevant to WhatsApp-style messaging because it combines rapid exchange with visual content that carries emotional and relational meaning. When users share photos in a medium that feels immediate and personal, they develop stronger expectations about how that content should be handled \cite{daft_lengel_mrt_1986,short_williams_christie_1976}.Therefore withdrawing consent may be experienced not simply as changing access but as a socially delicate act implying mistrust or a shift in relational boundaries. Hence revocation in WhatsApp-style photo sharing needs to be understood in terms of interpersonal meaning as well as technical control.

\section{The Concept of Consent}

\subsection{The Limits of Formal Consent}
Under the GDPR, consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, and withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent; where consent is withdrawn and no other legal basis remains, the right to erasure may also be engaged \cite{gdpr_2016,politou_2018}. Yet formal compliance does not necessarily make consent meaningful in practice. Baruh and Popescu argue that privacy self-management based on notice and choice places too much responsibility on individuals, even though data practices are often complex, and difficult to resist in practice \cite{baruh_big_2017}. In such settings, consent may be legally valid without being substantively meaningful, creating a validity gap between formal agreement and real user control. They further show that users may respond in two limited ways: through partial withdrawal or avoidance when privacy risks feel unmanageable, or through assimilation, where users accept reduced control and rely on market-provided protections \cite{baruh_big_2017}.

Waldman extends this critique by showing that privacy is often operationalised narrowly within organisations. In his account, lawyers tend to reduce privacy to notice and choice, while engineers often equate it with security or encryption, leaving limited space for more user-centred understandings of consent and control \cite{waldman_designing_nodate}. For the present topic, this suggests that effective revocation cannot depend only on formal consent or interface disclosure, but must be supported by designs that make control realistic and usable in practice.   

\subsection{Consent Models}

Consent in everyday messaging cannot be viewed solely as a single permission granted by an individual, since interactions develop over time through different relationships and are often conducted with a degree of loss of control. Traditional consent frameworks tend to assume that consent is static, singular, and transaction-like. However, this logic becomes unstable in ongoing interpersonal exchange, where the meaning of permission can shift after disclosure. As Burkhardt et al. suggest, meaningful consent depends not only on formal agreement but also on understanding and perceived control, while work on consent and revocation has also shown that decisions made at one moment cannot remain fully informed across the lifetime of data use \cite{burkhardt_privacy_2023,agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012}. This is why dynamic consent has been established as a alternative to static one time authorisation models that ask people to agree to future uses they may neither anticipate nor revisit \cite{schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022}. In messaging, these weaknesses are particularly visible because permissions can have multiple meanings over time and also may change depending upon how interactions evolve or what occurs subsequent to messages being delivered. 

The first weakness is temporal. Static models assume consent is given once and remains valid indefinitely, yet in messaging the meaning of permission shifts as conversations develop. Dynamic Consent responds to this by treating permission as revisable, emphasising ongoing engagement, granular choice, and adjustable preferences over time \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,lee_identifying_2023}. Wilbanks reinforces this by arguing that digital consent should begin an ongoing ethical relationship rather than conclude a one-time administrative step, and later work frames modifiable consent choices as central to user-centred data governance \cite{wilbanks_design_2018,narkhede_enhancing_2025}. However, Dynamic Consent improves most clearly the timing of permission, not post-disclosure control. In everyday messaging, being able to revise a preference does not necessarily mean being able to affect content that has already been delivered and moved beyond the sender's immediate control.

The second weakness is that revisability is not the same as withdrawal. Even if a user can update preferences going forward, the harder question is whether permission already granted can be meaningfully taken back. Revocable Consent foregrounds this problem. The literature usefully distinguishes between revocability as a normative requirement and revocation as a practically enforceable outcome. Consent and revocation have been framed as ongoing controls, with informed revocation emphasising that users should understand the forms of withdrawal available to them \cite{agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012}. Related technical work treats withdrawal as a change in the scope of authorised access where users should be able to specify who may access data, for what purposes, and for how long, while formal models of consent evolution represent revocation by closing a previously open interval of collection or access rather than undoing prior disclosure \cite{merlec_smart_2021,robol_consent_2023}. This distinction is especially important for photo sharing, where a sender may be able to end future authorised access without being able to retract a photo that has already been viewed, saved, or copied. Revocation in messaging is therefore best understood as limiting future legitimacy and platform-supported access, not as guaranteeing recovery of the original disclosure. This problem is reinforced by the broader notice-and-consent literature, which shows that even ostensibly reversible interfaces can fail to produce meaningful control when the design of choice itself is manipulative or unclear \cite{nouwens_dark_2020}.

Two further models address narrower weaknesses. Retroactive consent makes the timing of authorisation explicit, allowing consent and withdrawal to be analysed in terms of whether their effects apply to past or future data practices \cite{robol_consent_2023}. Ephemeral consent takes a different approach by limiting persistence prospectively, treating permission as bounded and revisable rather than indefinite
\cite{im_yes_2021}. Yet both remain partial. Once content has been copied, remembered, or redistributed by others, neither temporal expiry nor after-the-fact adjustment can fully restore sender control.

Seen comparatively, these models should not be treated as mutually exclusive competitors, but as attempts to repair different weaknesses in conventional consent. Dynamic Consent responds most clearly to temporality; Revocable Consent foregrounds withdrawal and reversibility; retroactive approaches sharpen how timing affects authorisation; and ephemeral approaches seek to limit persistence before problems arise. What unites them is a shared difficulty in accounting for messaging as a social practice in which the meaning and consequences of disclosure continue to evolve after the moment of sending \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,robol_consent_2023,politou_2018,im_yes_2021}. The central problem is therefore not simply lack of choice, but the challenge of aligning  revocability and downstream loss of control within a single model.

For this reason, affirmative and ongoing consent offers the strongest conceptual direction for this dissertation. Im et al. describe affirmative consent as voluntary, informed, revertible, specific, and unburdensome, providing a framework that treats permission as active and revisable \cite{im_yes_2021}. These principles fit messaging more closely than one-off models because they better reflect how consent is negotiated in unfolding interaction. At the same time, they should not be overstated as a technical solutions to screenshots, retention, or redistribution. Their value lies in guiding responsible design that allows permissions to be revisited and renegotiated, without assuming full post-sharing control is possible.  

This issue becomes even more complex in photo sharing, where consent may extend beyond a single sender-recipient relationship. 

Taken together, the literature suggests that the main weakness of conventional consent models is not simply insufficient granularity, but their inability to account for the ongoing social life of shared content. Improved models repair important dimensions of this problem, but they still encounter hard limits once messages and photos enter interpersonal networks where recipients can retain, reinterpret, copy, or redistribute them. For everyday messaging, meaningful consent is therefore best understood not as a completed transaction but as an ongoing and socially situated process whose practical force depends on both relational norms and the technical limits of post-sharing control.


\section{Consent in Instant Messaging and Photo Sharing}

\subsection{Multi-Party Privacy Conflicts (MPCs)}

Multi-party privacy conflicts arise when one photo affects several people with different privacy interests, making individualised consent models inadequate. In the photo-sharing literature, MPCs are generally understood as situations in which the person who decides to share content is not the only person who bears the privacy consequences of that disclosure. One user’s act of sharing can expose others depicted in the image, even where those others have had no meaningful opportunity to approve, refuse, or shape that disclosure. Cherubini et al. show that this problem is especially remarkable for co-owned content such as group photos, while Liu et al.’s survey of image privacy similarly suggests that privacy in online image sharing is shaped not only by image content itself but also by relationships, context, and multi-party interaction \cite{cherubini_2021, liu_privacy_2023}. Applied to instant messaging, this means that a shared photo may simultaneously involve the sender, the recipient, and any depicted co-subjects, each of whom may hold different expectations about whether the image should be sent, retained, forwarded, or later withdrawn. Consent in photo sharing is therefore not only something that may need to be revisited over time, as argued in the previous subsection, but also something distributed across multiple stakeholders from the outset, which makes sender-only models of permission normatively and practically incomplete \cite{cherubini_2021, liu_privacy_2023}.

The literature proposes several responses to multi-party privacy conflicts, but these approaches often manage the problem more effectively than they resolve it. Cherubini et al. distinguish between precautionary mechanisms, which prevent or constrain sharing before disclosure, and dissuasion mechanisms, which seek to discourage risky sharing by prompting reflection on possible consequences \cite{cherubini_2021}. They find that participants generally prefer precautionary mechanisms because these provide stronger protection for affected parties and reduce uncertainty for uploaders, although they also introduce friction and depend on identifying co-owners in advance \cite{cherubini_2021}. This limitation is reflected in technical proposals. Xu et al. (2017) propose a system in which people appearing in a photo can be identified and involved in the sharing decision, but this depends on a privacy-preserving facial-recognition process to detect co-owners in the first place \cite{xu_my_2017}. Again in another paper, Xu et al. (2019), similarly develop a trust-based privacy-preserving sharing model that allows selective protection of co-owned content, however the approach still treats the problem largely through pre-sharing optimisation and does not fully incorporate direct negotiation among all affected parties \cite{xu_trust-based_2019}. Zhang et al. (2023) extend this critique by arguing that privacy-preserving frameworks often define ownership too narrowly and do not fully address the risks introduced by subsequent forwarding and cross-platform propagation \cite{zhang_go-sharing_2023}. 
When combined, these methods demonstrate that co-ownership cannot be reduced to sender-side privacy settings. However, they are mostly pre-sharing, system-centered, or prediction-based, and as a result, they suffer when images become technically and socially mobile.\cite{cherubini_2021, xu_my_2017, xu_trust-based_2019, zhang_go-sharing_2023}.

Multi-party privacy conflicts therefore show that consent revocation in messaging is not simply a matter of allowing one sender to change their mind, but a broader problem of shared stakes and weakening control once images begin to circulate \cite{cherubini_2021, zhang_go-sharing_2023}.

\subsection{Persistence, Recipient Control, and the Limits of Revocation}

Multi-party privacy conflicts show that control over a photo may already be shared before it is sent however the next limit is structural. In instant messaging, revocation does not operate as ongoing access control after delivery, because the system’s basic function is to transmit content to a recipient-controlled device rather than to preserve sender authority over it indefinitely. For that reason, revocation is best understood as prospective and partial. Once a photo has been delivered, a platform may still alter later in-app availability, but it cannot guarantee retrieval of the original disclosure. This is consistent with earlier work on revocation as a forward-looking rather than fully retroactive mechanism, and with Schnitzler et al.’s observation that deleting messages from a recipient’s history is substantially harder than deleting them locally, even in closed messaging ecosystems \cite{agrafiotis_consent_revocation_2012, politou_2018, schnitzler_2020}.

WhatsApp provides a useful case because its privacy architecture and its revocation features point in opposite directions. On the one hand, WhatsApp emphasises end-to-end encryption and states in its UK Privacy Policy that messages are typically stored on users’ devices rather than on its servers once delivery has occurred, on the other hand, this model means that post-delivery control is necessarily limited \cite{whatsapp_e2e_encryption, whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. Deletion, disappearing messages, and view-once media should therefore be interpreted as bounded controls over later visibility, not as guarantees of erasure. WhatsApp’s own help documentation states that recipients may see a message before deletion succeeds, that unsuccessful deletion may not be flagged to the sender, and that iOS recipients may still have media saved in Photos even after the message is deleted from the chat \cite{whatsapp_delete_messages}. Likewise, disappearing messages only affect new messages and can be kept in the chat, while unopened view-once media can still be restored from backups. By limiting export and auto-download, even more recent safeguards like Advanced Chat Privacy are said to help in preventing content from being taken outside of WhatsApp, which shows that these measures reduce rather than completely remove persistence pathways. \cite{whatsapp_disappearing_messages, whatsapp_view_once, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy_blog_2025}.

The weakness of revocation is not only technical but also interpretive. Schnitzler et al. show that users do not always correctly understand what a deletion action actually does, and that clearer interface explanations improve their ability to assess deletion outcomes; in their study, participants in the WhatsApp condition understood message deletion more accurately than those in the Skype condition, partly because WhatsApp more clearly distinguishes local from broader deletion effects \cite{schnitzler_2020}. Furthermore, the same study also shows that deletion notices and other residual traces matter socially. Participants were sometimes surprised not by whether deletion occurred, but by the notification that a message had been deleted, and some explicitly associated such traces with mistrust or ambiguity. In this sense, revocation in WhatsApp photo sharing is also an expectation-management problem. If interface cues suggest stronger control than the system can actually deliver, users may overestimate what deletion has achieved and if revocation is made too visibly traceable, it may also generate interpersonal friction while not restoring privacy \cite{schnitzler_2020}.


\subsection{The Screenshot Threat}

The screenshot threat matters because it creates a form of persistence outside the platform's own retention settings. WhatsApp's privacy-oriented features, including disappearing messages, `view once' media, and advanced chat privacy, can limit some later visibility or sharing within the app, but they do not fully govern what a recipient can do after viewing content \cite{whatsapp_disappearing_messages, whatsapp_view_once, whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy, whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. As Heath et al. note, even disappearing-message systems leave open preservation routes such as forwarding, screenshotting, copying, saving, or photographing content before it disappears \cite{heath_2023}. As Ingber argues, screenshots turn part of a conversation into a new file on the recipient's device, making it easier to circulate beyond the original exchange \cite{ingber_2025}. Screenshotting therefore creates a parallel persistence route that sits partly outside the logic of in-app revocation and ephemerality. Even if the original message becomes less visible within WhatsApp, a copied representation may continue to exist elsewhere. More broadly, image-privacy scholarship identifies the recipient side as a continuing source of privacy risk once content is already in circulation, because recipients may retain or recirculate images beyond the sender's intended audience \cite{liu_privacy_2023}.

Critically, screenshots are both technical workarounds and socially meaningful acts. Ingber distinguishes screenshot collection from screenshot sharing, showing that both generate distinct privacy harms by moving co-owned information into a new context without notice or consent \cite{ingber_2025}. Since affirmative consent must be specific \cite{im_yes_2021}, permission to share a photo with one recipient does not automatically translate to agreeing it to copy or recirculate. Screenshots therefore make revocation not only technically weak but normatively contested. This means recipients may treat capture as harmless remembering or legitimate reuse, while senders may understand it as a breach of containment and trust. Screenshot risk should accordingly be treated as a central challenge for any interface that presents revocation or ephemerality as meaningful user control \cite{ingber_2025, heath_2023, liu_privacy_2023}.

\section{Human Computer Interaction and Interface Design}
HCI research shows that consent and revocation controls often fail in practice when users cannot easily find or confidently use privacy settings in fast-paced sharing contexts \cite{bourgeus_vandercruysse_2024,wilbanks_design_2018}. This problem is closely connected to whether privacy options are presented transparently and neutrally, allowing users to act on them meaningfully rather than being guided toward platform-preferred outcomes. \cite{nouwens_dark_2020}.

\subsection{Interface Failures and Manipulative Patterns}
Even when platforms formally provide privacy and consent controls, HCI research shows these mechanisms often fail to produce meaningful user control at the interface level. This is an issue that becomes especially consequential in messaging-based photo sharing, where disclosures are often fast, and difficult to reverse once an image leaves the sender’s device. Classic notice-and-choice approaches are frequently ineffective because notices are not usable or useful in practice and are frequently treated as something bolted onto a system \cite{schaub_notice_2015}. In messaging and social media settings, this becomes visible as concrete usability failures. Expert evaluations using privacy-focused heuristics find that the ability to revoke consent is among the most problematic areas across popular apps, and that privacy information is inconsistently placed in menus in ways that undermine core usability principles such as consistency \cite{albesher_2021}. For photo sharing, the implication is straightforward: users cannot withdraw consent meaningfully if image-related controls (e.g., deletion, visibility, sharing restrictions) are hard to find and compare, and ambiguous about what they actually change—particularly when users are acting quickly in-chat and assume a “delete” action restores privacy.

This problem is intensified when interface designs do not merely confuse users but actively steer them toward platform-preferred outcomes. Empirical work on consent dialogs shows that “consent” interfaces commonly use design tactics that encourage acceptance of defaults (e.g., making ``accept'' more prominent, hiding configuration behind additional steps...), so the banner functions more like a notification than a genuine control mechanism \cite{bermejo_fernandez_2021}. Analyses of GDPR-oriented consent pop-ups likewise argue that dark-pattern strategies can be used to circumvent the regulation’s intent ``by design,'' and that rights-protecting regulation is weakened if interface design is left unaddressed \cite{soe_2020}. From a legal/HCI perspective, this steering is especially problematic because valid consent requires a meaningful choice and the ability to withdraw consent, and designs that make ``accept'' more salient than ``reject'' or that hide privacy-relevant options undermine that balance \cite{gray_etal_2021}. Experimental evidence also suggests that cookie-consent regimes often do not work as intended: participants frequently select privacy-unfriendly options and report low perceived control, raising concerns that interface-level nudges and defaults can hollow out the ideal of deliberative consent \cite{grasl_dark}. For WhatsApp-style photo sharing, the key implication is that revocation controls must not only exist but must be reachable and resistant to interface choices that overstate user agency or make withdrawal harder than acceptance.

\subsection{Interactive Design for Meaningful and Revocable Consent}
Constructive HCI work suggests that meaningful consent is best supported when it is designed as an explicit, understandable interaction. Wilbanks argues that in e-consent, interface design shapes whether people understand what they agree to, so consent should promote comprehension instead of sole completion. \cite{wilbanks_design_2018}. Relatedly, affirmative-consent work, frames consent as requiring conditions such as being informed, specific, and revertible, which translates at the interface level into making actions intentional, consequences legible, and outcomes visible \cite{im_yes_2021}. On mobile devices in particular, alternative consent designs that go beyond a standard tap-through can increase attention to what is being agreed to \cite{lindegren_evaluation_2021}. For photo sharing, these insights imply that consent-relevant controls should be embedded into the moment of sending in a way users can notice under time pressure, and should provide clear feedback so users can tell what has changed after they act.

The same interactional logic extends from initial permission to later revision and withdrawal. Schaub et al.\ emphasise that the effectiveness of privacy notices depends on design dimensions such as timing and delivery context, supporting the use of just-in-time and contextually placed cues rather than relying on static policies \cite{schaub_notice_2015}. Where revocation is difficult to operationalise after disclosure, HCI work on multiparty privacy conflicts shows that design can intervene earlier through precautionary mechanisms (e.g., requiring collaboration/approval) or dissuasive mechanisms (e.g., friction or warnings that prompt reconsideration) \cite{cherubini_2021}, these approaches are especially relevant to photo sharing because they can reduce avoidable disclosures before images become socially and technically mobile. Dynamic-consent literature strengthens this orientation by treating permissions as reviewable and updateable over time via interface-supported communication and preference management \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022,lee_identifying_2023}. Technical proposals for dynamic consent management further highlight capabilities such as explicit consent choices, access to consent history, and withdrawal support across a data lifecycle \cite{merlec_smart_2021}, hough adoption-focused reviews also note practical barriers such as consent fatigue and implementation complexity \cite{lee_identifying_2023}. For WhatsApp-style photo sharing, the design takeaway is not to promise perfect post-sharing control, but to build interactions that help users act deliberately at sharing time, understand the practical scope of revocation, and revisit decisions later through clear controls.

\section{Empirical Usability of Current Platforms}
Empirical evaluations suggest that revocation-related privacy controls are frequently available in principle but weak in usable effectiveness. In a STRAP-based expert heuristic evaluation of WhatsApp, Twitter, and Snapchat, Albesher and Alhussain identify the ``ability to revoke consent'' as the most notable problem area across all three apps, noting that revocation is conditional or unreliable and that privacy settings are presented inconsistently, undermining basic usability principles \cite{albesher_2021}. Revocation therefore tends to be a high-severity usability weakness, not simply a missing feature.

WhatsApp illustrates this concretely. Platform features such as message deletion, disappearing messages, view-once media, and chat-level protections can reduce some forms of persistence, but they operate as mitigations rather than guaranteed erasure \cite{whatsapp_delete_messages,whatsapp_disappearing_messages,whatsapp_view_once,whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy,whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025}. Forensic analysis by Heath et al.\ further shows that disappearing-message behaviour varies across platforms and storage locations, that content can be preserved through replying, forwarding, or screenshotting before expiry, and that message timers and residual artefacts can remain in application databases \cite{heath_2023}. Overall, existing platforms provide controls that gesture toward revocation, but empirical and platform evidence suggests these controls are weaker in practice than their presence implies.

\section{Research Gap and Summary}
Taken together, the literature reviewed in this chapter shows that consent revocation in messaging is neither a purely technical function nor a fully solved interface problem. Earlier sections establish that revocation is socially negotiated, only partially effective after delivery and difficult to translate into interaction models that users can understand and act on in practice. The remaining research gaps therefore concern not whether revocation matters, but how it can be operationalised, scoped, and evaluated in WhatsApp-like photo-sharing contexts.

\subsection{RG1: Translating dynamic/ongoing consent into usable messaging UI}
The chapter has shown that dynamic, ongoing, and revocable consent are conceptually better suited to messaging than one-off authorisation, because they treat permission as revisable over time rather than exhausted at the moment of sending \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,schuler_scott_dynamic_consent_2022,lee_identifying_2023,im_yes_2021}. However, the literature reviewed here provides much stronger conceptual and governance-level accounts than concrete guidance on how such models become low-friction, intelligible interaction flows within routine messaging. The unresolved issue, therefore, is how to express ongoing consent through usable controls and in-context permission management for everyday photo sharing without overwhelming users or overstating what such controls can achieve \cite{merlec_smart_2021,wilbanks_design_2018}.

\subsection{RG2: Post-delivery control is partial and easily misunderstood under real redistribution conditions}
The chapter also shows that real platforms already offer deletion and ephemerality features, but that these operate only as bounded post-delivery mitigations rather than enforceable recall. In WhatsApp-like systems, revocation becomes structurally weaker once content reaches recipient-controlled devices, and users do not always interpret deletion cues or feature scope accurately \cite{politou_2018,schnitzler_2020,whatsapp_delete_messages,whatsapp_disappearing_messages,whatsapp_view_once}. This problem intensifies under actual redistribution conditions, because forwarding, saving, screenshotting, and other capture practices create persistence routes beyond platform-scoped revocation \cite{heath_2023,ingber_2025}. The remaining gap is therefore not simply that current controls are imperfect, but that there is still no robust model for post-delivery control that remains user-comprehensible once circulation extends beyond the original in-app exchange.

\subsection{RG3: Multi-party privacy and fairness are under-supported in revocation interaction design}
Throughout this chapter, privacy has been described as relational, context-specific, and often multi-party. So, once a photo is shared, senders, recipients, and depicted co-subjects can all have legitimate but competing stakes in how it is retained or circulated \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,nissenbaum_contextual_2011,cherubini_2021,liu_privacy_2023}. However, existing messaging platforms largely encode revocation as a sender-centred action, offering little support for co-subject agency, negotiation, or explanations that make revocation clear to all affected parties. Therefore, the unresolved issue is about equally distributing the control power between stakeholders. The literature currently lacks a clear interaction model for revocation that balance stakeholder transparency while minimizing unnecessary interpersonal conflict as well as social sensitivity when exchanging such information.  \cite{cherubini_2021,ingber_2025}.

\subsection{RG4: Limited WhatsApp-specific empirical understanding of revocable photo-permission expectations}
The reviewed literature gives a strong account of why WhatsApp is an important case: its controls are widely used, bounded in scope, and socially meaningful in ways that affect trust, interpretation, and downstream sharing \cite{whatsapp_privacy_policy_uk_2025,whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy,whatsapp_delete_messages,schnitzler_2020,ingber_2025}. It also provides platform documentation, heuristic usability evaluation, and evidence about screenshot or deletion effects \cite{albesher_2021,schnitzler_2020,heath_2023,ingber_2025}. What remains under-evidenced, however, is what WhatsApp users themselves expect from revocable photo permissions in ordinary relational contexts. This includes what forms of control they would understand, trust, accept, or regard as socially fair. The final gap is therefore empirical and context-specific, centring on user expectations rather than on the technical existence of platform features.

\subsection{Summary}
Together, these gaps show that the central problem is not simply whether revocation exists, but whether it can be translated into interaction models that are understandable and grounded in the expectations of WhatsApp users. They therefore motivate the need for a messaging-appropriate mechanism that supports socially usable, and realistically scoped revocation of photo access in WhatsApp-like contexts.
