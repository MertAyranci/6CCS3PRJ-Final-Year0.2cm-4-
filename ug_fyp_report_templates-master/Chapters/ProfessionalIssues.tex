\section{Legal Issues}

The legal dimension of this project concerns both the handling of research data during evaluation and the extent to which a consent-revocation interface can realistically support safer photo sharing in messaging environments. In the evaluation, the study used fictional or stock-based example imagery and scenario-based tasks rather than participants' own intimate content, and the prototype itself was implemented as a front-end simulation with in-memory state only, without real user accounts, server-side storage, or a persistent database. This meant the project was designed to minimise the collection and retention of personal data. In that sense, the approach is aligned with the data-minimisation and research-data handling principles associated with the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, while avoiding any stronger claim that the prototype has been independently verified as legally compliant in the way a deployed commercial platform would need to be \cite{gdpr_2016, dpa_2018}.

At the design level, the UK GDPR is relevant not because it was written specifically for consent revocation in private messaging, but because it provides a useful benchmark for what meaningful consent and withdrawal should look like in digital systems. Under the GDPR, consent should be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and withdrawal should be as easy as giving consent \cite{gdpr_2016}. This principle supports the aim of the Smart Photos prototype, which was designed to make permissions clearer before sharing and revocation more accessible after sharing. However, as Politou et al.\ \cite{politou_2018} note, revocation operates prospectively rather than retroactively, meaning that later withdrawal cannot guarantee complete erasure once data have already been viewed, copied, saved or redistributed. This is especially relevant in messaging contexts such as WhatsApp-style photo sharing, where screenshots, external captures, or recipient-side saving remain possible even if the platform changes future in-app access. The project also relates broadly to the Online Safety Act 2023, since it explores interface mechanisms intended to reduce harm, improve user control, and support safer handling of sensitive content, but it should still be understood as an academic proof-of-concept rather than a system that itself fulfils statutory platform duties \cite{online_safety_act_2023}. Finally, although the prototype deliberately adopts familiar WhatsApp-like interaction patterns for evaluative realism, it remains an original student implementation and should not imply any endorsement by, or affiliation with, Meta.
\section{Ethical Issues}
Researching consent revocation in private photo sharing involves a privacy-sensitive topic, since participants are asked to reflect on changing permissions, interpersonal boundaries, and the potential misuse of shared images in messaging environments. Although the project does not focus on explicitly sexual content, engaging human participants still required careful ethical safeguards to protect their rights, privacy, and wellbeing. In line with the British Psychological Society's guidance for internet-mediated research, the evaluation was designed to minimise discomfort and avoid unnecessary disclosure of personal material \cite{bps_2021}. Participation was restricted to adults aged 18--24, informed consent was obtained at the start of each session, and participants were told that they could skip questions, stop the interview at any time, and choose whether or not to be audio-recorded. To reduce ethical risk further, the study did not use participants' own private photos, message histories, or real consent disputes. Instead, it relied on fictional example photos, screenshot-based scenarios, and a front-end prototype designed solely for evaluation. Interview recordings were transcribed for analysis, and participant data were anonymised in the reporting process. This approach is also consistent with value-sensitive design principles, which argue that systems should be developed with attention to human values such as privacy, autonomy, and informed control rather than technical functionality alone \cite{friedman_hendry_2019}. Ethically, the prototype was therefore designed not only to explore stronger user control, but also to avoid overstating what such control can achieve once a photo has already been delivered.

\section{Social Issues}
Photo sharing in private messaging is not only a technical exchange between a sender and a recipient, but also a social process shaped by trust, audience, and changing interpersonal boundaries. Petronio and Child \cite{petronio_cpm_2020} argue that privacy is negotiated through ongoing boundary management rather than fixed at a single moment, which is highly relevant to this project because consent may change after a photo has already been shared. The social scope of the problem is also wider than a single sender-recipient pair. Cherubini et al. \cite{cherubini_2021} show that photo sharing can create multi-party privacy conflicts when other depicted people are affected by a disclosure, and this becomes even more complex when a photo is forwarded to secondary recipients. In response, the Smart Photos prototype was designed to make these social relationships more visible by showing forwarding context, propagating permission changes across forwarded copies, and allowing depicted co-subjects to request revocation. These features could support more responsible photo-sharing norms by making consent changes clearer, acknowledging that photos may affect more than one person, and encouraging users to treat redistribution as a socially significant action rather than a neutral one. However, the evaluation also showed that revocation can still be interpreted as mistrust, annoyance, or offence depending on the relationship and context. For this reason, the project should be understood as one contribution toward safer and more accountable communication practices, rather than as a complete solution to the broader social problems surrounding privacy, redistribution, and interpersonal trust in digital messaging.


\section{Professional Issues}

Throughout this project, the work was guided by the BCS Code of Conduct and Code of Good Practice, particularly its emphasis on acting in the public interest, maintaining professional competence and integrity, fulfilling relevant legal and ethical responsibilities, and contributing responsibly to the profession \cite{bcs_2022}. These principles are directly relevant to a project concerned with privacy-sensitive photo sharing, since poorly designed consent features could mislead users, overstate the level of protection available, or create a false sense of security. In response, the Smart Photos model was developed as a research prototype grounded in literature, iterative design reasoning, and interview-based evaluation, with findings reported cautiously rather than presented as proof of a production-ready solution. This was especially important because the project deliberately focused on interaction design rather than real platform infrastructure, and therefore does not claim to solve technical issues such as screenshots, external-device capture, encryption compatibility, or cross-device synchronisation.

Professional integrity in this project also required transparency and accountability in both implementation and reporting. The prototype was evaluated through a defined research process, supported by automated testing and explicit discussion of limitations, including the constraints of screenshot-based evaluation and the restricted scope of a front-end simulation. Rather than presenting revocation as complete control, the report consistently acknowledges that post-sharing permissions are partial and prospective, and it treats expectation management as a core design responsibility. This approach aligns with broader computing ethics arguments that professional responsibility involves not only building useful systems, but also communicating their limits honestly and considering their wider social consequences \cite{gotterbarn_2018}. More broadly, the project contributes to the profession by offering evidence-based design recommendations for platform providers, educators, and policymakers on how revocable permissions, forwarding awareness, and co-subject governance might be handled more responsibly in messaging environments.
