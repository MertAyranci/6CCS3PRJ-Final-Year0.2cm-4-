\chapter{Design \& Specification}

\section{Brief}
This chapter presents the design and specification of the proposed consent revocation system for photo sharing in WhatsApp. It explains the interaction design and system specification, drawing on the requirements and literature. The chapter first introduces the Smart Photos concept and design rationale, before covering the specification in sections 4.3 and 4.4. 

\section{Design Rationale and Principles}
The design of \textbf{Smart Photos} is grounded in the central argument of this dissertation: consent in messaging-based photo sharing should not be treated as a one-off permission granted at the moment of sending, but as an ongoing and revisable process. In the proposed model, each shared photo is treated as a photo associated with a persistent permission state that can specify whether it may be forwarded, saved, viewed once or accessed for a limited period. These permissions are not exhausted at disclosure, but remain reviewable and revisable through the interface after sending. The design principles set out below are informed by Communication Privacy Management, Contextual Integrity, revised consent models, and privacy-focused HCI research \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011,nissenbaum_contextual_2011,im_yes_2021,wilbanks_design_2018,schaub_notice_2015}. They provide the conceptual basis for the structure of the model and the prioritisation of particular interaction choices.

\subsection{Dynamic and Revocable Consent by Default}
The first design principle is that consent should be treated as dynamic and revocable by default. This follows directly from the literature reviewed in Chapter 2, which shows that one-off consent models are poorly suited to interpersonal messaging, where disclosures unfold over time and may later feel inappropriate as relationships, or expectations change \cite{kaye_dynamic_2015,im_yes_2021}. In CPM terms, sharing a photo does not eliminate the need for later boundary management; but, it creates a situation in which privacy rules may need to be revisited and renegotiated after disclosure \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,child_petronio_cmc_privacy_2011}. 

For that reason, the design does not treat photo sending as an irreversible transfer of control. Instead, it is structured around the idea that a photo can remain associated with a modifiable permission state, allowing the sender to determine conditions of access before sending and to revise those conditions afterwards. This principle underpins the decision to support both pre-send configuration and post-send permission management in the prototype. This is how the prototype operationalises the dissertation's first research gap by translating the literature on dynamic and ongoing consent into a messaging-oriented interaction model that remains reviewable after disclosure.

\subsection{Expectation Management over Absolute Control}
The second principle is that the interface should communicate realistic and bounded forms of control without implying that full revocation is possible. The literature reviewed in Chapter 2 shows consistently that revocation in messaging environments is prospective and partial: once content has been delivered, viewed, or saved through screenshots or external devices, no interface can guarantee complete retrieval or universal deletion \cite{politou_2018,schnitzler_2020,heath_2023,ingber_2025}. This limitation is especially important in WhatsApp-like systems, where platform-level controls can affect later in-app visibility but cannot fully govern what has already been retained on recipient-controlled devices \cite{whatsapp_delete_messages,whatsapp_disappearing_messages,whatsapp_view_once}.

This principle therefore rejects any design framing that would overstate the application's power. Within the \textbf{Smart Photos} model, revocation is treated primarily as a mechanism for modifying or withdrawing future platform-mediated access and redistribution. The model never guarantees complete deletion after delivery. This makes expectation management a core design responsibility. It justifies the inclusion of screenshot warnings and wording that emphasises limited but meaningful control. In practical terms, the goal is to help users form a more accurate mental model of what permission changes can and cannot achieve. 

\subsection{Minimal Social Friction}
A third design principle is that revocation and permission change should be communicated in a way that minimises unnecessary interpersonal tension. The literature reviewed earlier shows that revocation is as much a social act as a technical one. Under CPM, changing access after disclosure may be interpreted as a renegotiation of privacy boundaries, while work on social presence and media richness suggests that changes to communication permissions can carry relational signals such as mistrust or distancing \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,daft_lengel_mrt_1986,short_williams_christie_1976,ishii_etal_2019}. Empirical work on deletion cues further suggests that visible traces such as deletion notices may themselves become socially consequential, sometimes producing ambiguity or discomfort rather than simply clarifying what happened \cite{schnitzler_2020}.

For that reason, the design aims to reduce friction without hiding the fact that a permission change has occurred. This means using neutral, system-mediated wording and avoiding interface patterns that imply blame. The aim is not to hide revocation, but to present it in a way that it limits the possibility of unnecessary tension. This principle is particularly important for recipient-facing notices and status changes, where the interface must remain clear about changing access while avoiding designs that frame revocation as punitive or suspicious.

\subsection{Fairness Between Parties }
Although the project prioritises sender-side control, the design rationale does not treat consent management as a purely sender-centred matter. Both CPM and Contextual Integrity suggest that once content has been shared, the expectations of other parties also matter, even where the sender retains a legitimate interest in revising access \cite{petronio_cpm_2020,nissenbaum_contextual_2011}. A sender may view revocation as a reasonable attempt to reassert privacy boundaries, while a recipient may interpret the same action as a change to previously granted access. This tension does not mean the sender should lose the ability to revise permissions, but it does mean that recipient-facing states and notices must be understandable. Although the immediate interaction in the prototype centres on the sender and both types of recipients, the same concern with fairness also extends to photos that involve depicted co-subjects whose interests may be affected by later access or redistribution.

The design therefore balances sender agency with recipient understanding: the system shows what has changed, and both recipients and co-subjects can ask for renewed or revoked access while the sender keeps the final decision. The design reflects the literature's view of consent as a socially negotiated process and responds to it.

\subsection{Consistency with WhatsApp's Existing Interaction Patterns}
The final principle is that new revocation and permission features should remain consistent with the interaction patterns users already associate with WhatsApp-like messaging. HCI research reviewed in Chapter 2 shows that privacy controls often fail because they are presented inconsistently, or are difficult to interpret during fast-paced use \cite{albesher_2021,schaub_notice_2015,wilbanks_design_2018}. In messaging contexts, where actions are often taken quickly and with limited deliberation, recognisable interaction patterns are especially important for first-use clarity and adoption.

For that reason, the design does not attempt to invent an entirely separate privacy-management environment detached from ordinary messaging behaviour. Instead, it follows familiar WhatsApp-like interaction conventions such as photo-level action menus, recognisable icons, in-chat state cues, and lightweight bottom-sheet style controls. This principle supports learnability and consistency, and helps ensure that the proposed system feels like a plausible extension of an existing messaging platform. 

\section{Functional Specification}

\subsection{Pre-send Permission Configuration}
To achieve \textbf{FR1} and \textbf{FR2}, the prototype places photo-permission controls directly within the sender’s sharing flow which brings privacy decisions into the moment of sharing itself. From the sender’s chat interface, the user can open a photo picker, choose an image from media library, device files, or camera input, and then either send it immediately with default settings or open a dedicated composer for permission-based sharing. This keeps permission decisions close to the moment of disclosure while preserving a lightweight path for routine sharing.

When the sender chooses to send a photo with revocable permissions, the prototype opens a dedicated pre-send composer. Within this composer, the sender can define the photo’s initial permission state before it enters the chat. It allows the sender to set forwarding, view-once, downloading, co-subject marking, and time-limited permissions. Time-limited access is configured through a duration selector. Also, the sender can add a caption before sending. Therefore, the act of sending, function as a creation of a photo message with an attached permission state.

To achieve \textbf{FR2}, the pre-send flow also includes an explicit risk cue that communicates the limits of later control before disclosure takes place. The composer shows a short warning that screenshots and saved copies cannot be undone. This does not attempt to prevent screenshotting or local retention technically but instead, it frames such risks as structural limits of platform-level control and makes them visible before the photo is shared. As a result, the pre-send interaction supports more informed permission-setting by combining configurable restrictions with an explicit reminder that later revocation cannot fully reverse all forms of recipient-side capture.


\subsection{Post-send Permission Management}
To achieve \textbf{FR3}, the prototype keeps permission management reachable after a photo has already been delivered through contextual, photo-level controls within the chat. Each sent photo includes a small action button in the top corner of the message bubble which opens an in-chat menu. The sender can access both general photo actions and a dedicated revocation-permissions view. Through these post-send controls, the sender can revoke or restore visibility, allow or block forwarding, allow or block downloading, and add or remove a time limit for an existing photo. In this way, post-send control is treated as an ongoing interaction tied to the individual photo message.

To achieve \textbf{FR5}, these changes operate on a live consent state associated with each shared photo. Because these permissions remain attached to the source photo, the prototype can update the state of an existing message rather than replacing it with a new one. The adjacent permissions panel shows the selected photo with its current status, allowing the sender to inspect it before making changes.

To achieve \textbf{FR4}, the prototype also renders these permission states clearly within the chat interface for all relevant participants. Beneath each photo, the interface displays compact state labels which indicates the current permission state of the photo. When access is revoked, the recipient-facing interface replaces the visible photo with an inline placeholder indicating that the photo is no longer available in the current chat. When a photo is configured as view-once, recipients open it in a full-screen view that becomes unavailable after closing. These states within the chat interface make permission changes legible in the conversation.

To achieve \textbf{FR6}, permission-related actions are accompanied by lightweight but persistent feedback within the same interface. After sending, the sender is shown a short summary of the most recent actions in a separate panel. More generally, the outcome of permission changes is reflected immediately through updated state chips under the photo and any visible change in recipient-facing presentation, such as revoked placeholders or expiry countdowns. Instead of using separate confirmation dialogs, the prototype makes post-send actions understandable by updating the same chat and control interfaces used to manage the photo.

\subsection{Recipient Experience and Access Request}
To achieve \textbf{FR10}, recipient-facing state labels use neutral, system-like language so that revocation is communicated as a change in access which aims to minimise the social tension.

To achieve \textbf{FR11}, the prototype also allows the recipient to request renewed access when a previously shared photo is no longer available. 
The recipient can request access with an optional explanatory message from the action menu of the revoked photo. The request does not automatically restore access but, instead, it is routed to the sender side of the prototype as a pending request associated with the source photo.

The sender can then explicitly accept or reject that request. If the sender accepts, the prototype restores the photo’s access state for the corresponding source photo. If the sender rejects, the recipient is shown a short follow-up message indicating that the access request has been rejected. This is how the prototype treats access renewal not as an automatic technical reversal, but as a negotiated interaction in which the recipient can ask for renewed access while the sender retains control over the final decision.


\subsection{Forwarding and Redistribution Control}
To achieve \textbf{FR7}, \textbf{FR8} and \textbf{FR9}, the prototype treats forwarding as a permission-dependent action rather than as an unrestricted continuation of ordinary message sharing. A photo can only be forwarded when the sender's current permission state allows redistribution and the photo remains active. In the chat interface, this is reflected through the photo-level action menu. When the forwarding is blocked, the forwarding option is disabled and when it is permitted, the photo can be forwarded into the secondary-recipient chat. This means, the sender also has a role in managing redistribution.

To achieve \textbf{FR8}, forwarded content does not create a separate copy with an independent permission model. Instead, the forwarded message remains linked to the original photo through the same source-photo identifier and inherits the sender-defined permissions as represented in the interface. This means that forwarded content continues to reflect the original photo's visibility status and other active restrictions. If the sender later changes the permission state of the original photo, those changes are also reflected in the forwarded instance associated with that same source photo. The prototype therefore models forwarding as continued circulation under the original permission logic.

To achieve \textbf{FR9}, the prototype presents forwarded content in a separate secondary-recipient chat which makes the evaluation easier and helps distinguishing the original recipient's conversation from the downstream one. Within that forwarded view, the interface also indicates that restrictions from the original chat still apply, reinforcing that redistribution remains governed by the sender-defined permission state. 

\subsection{Co-Subject and Multi-Party Privacy Control}
To achieve \textbf{FR12} and \textbf{FR13}, the prototype extends the permission model beyond the sender/recipient pair by allowing the sender to mark, at send time, that a photo contains a co-subject. This is done through the pre-send composer, where the sender can enable a co-subject tag as part of the photo's initial permission state. Photos marked in this way then become visible within a dedicated co-subject view, where the depicted person is shown a clear indication that they appear in the photo. Rather than treating the image as sender-only content, the prototype represents certain photos as multi-party disclosures whose privacy implications extend beyond the original sender and recipient.
To achieve \textbf{FR13} more specifically, the co-subject is shown a dedicated card stating that they are depicted in the photo and may request revocation, making their involvement explicit.

To achieve \textbf{FR12} and \textbf{FR14}, the prototype also provides a co-subject request flow through which the depicted person can ask the sender to revoke the photo. This request is initiated from the co-subject's photo-level action menu and opens a small modal in which the co-subject can send a revocation request together with an optional message. The request is then routed to the sender side of the prototype as a pending item associated with the relevant source photo. Revocation is not applied automatically. The sender is given an explicit accept-or-reject decision interface in the sender-only message area. This keeps co-subject privacy management within a negotiated interaction model rather than reducing it to hidden system behaviour.

To achieve \textbf{FR15}, the outcome of a co-subject request is communicated through clear but non-blaming feedback. On the sender side, accepted and rejected requests are shown with short outcome labels indicating whether the photo has been revoked or the co-subject has been notified of rejection. If the sender rejects the request, the co-subject receives a follow-up message stating that the revocation request has been rejected. If the sender accepts, the photo's access state is updated without framing the change in accusatory terms. In this way, the prototype makes the outcome of co-subject intervention visible while still aiming to minimise unnecessary interpersonal friction.

To achieve \textbf{FR16}, accepting a co-subject revocation request changes the permission state of the source photo itself. Because forwarded copies remain linked to that source and inherit its state, co-subject-triggered revocations propagate into downstream redistribution contexts rather than stopping at the single original exchange.

To achieve \textbf{FR17}, the prototype makes the limits of co-subject protection explicit. Both the co-subject view and the revocation-request flow state that protection is in-app only and that screenshots or prior viewing cannot be fully undone, presenting these controls as meaningful but bounded without implying complete erasure after disclosure.

\section{Non-Functional Specification}

\subsection{Usability and Interaction Clarity}
To achieve \textbf{NFR1} and \textbf{NFR7}, the prototype embeds permission-related controls within familiar, WhatsApp-like interaction points such as the photo picker, pre-send composer, in-chat photo menu, adjacent permissions panel, and message-level state labels. This is intended to make the permission model understandable and easy to learn from first use while keeping its behaviour consistent with current messaging patterns.

To achieve \textbf{NFR2}, the interface supports quick completion of routine sharing tasks while introducing additional interaction only where privacy-sensitive reflection is useful. In the current prototype, a selected photo can either be sent directly with default settings or opened in a dedicated composer for permission-based sharing. This preserves a lightweight path for ordinary sharing, while still allowing the sender to configure photo permissions when more control is needed 

To achieve \textbf{NFR3}, consent and privacy choices are presented as explicit, interpretable options rather than as hidden or one-sided defaults. All pre-send permission controls are clearly labeled buttons in the same viewable panel, which will allow senders to turn on/off each button without having to go through through obscured submenus or being forced toward a single preferred configuration. Similarly, post-send actions are presented as explicit menu choices within the photo-level action flow. This keeps the interface relatively transparent about what options are available and avoids structuring privacy control as a hidden or manipulative interaction.

To achieve \textbf{NFR4}, the prototype uses clearly separated action menus, labelled permission buttons, compact state indicators beneath photo messages, and distinct placeholders for revoked content so that users can recognise the current state of a photo without having to infer it indirectly. This applies to all stakeholder views, where visible permission changes need to remain legible after disclosure.

To achieve \textbf{NFR5}, permission-related actions remain responsive within the local browser-based prototype so that changes in state appear immediately during interaction and demonstration. In the current implementation, permission updates are applied directly to the active photo state and are reflected at once in the chat view, the permissions panel, forwarded content, and recipient-facing or co-subject-facing states where relevant. This helps preserve a credible and continuous messaging flow, particularly when the sender revises permissions after sending and the resulting changes need to remain immediately visible to other participants.

\subsection{Socially Careful Communication}
To achieve \textbf{NFR6}, the prototype communicates all permission changes through neutral, system-mediated language that avoids unnecessary blame or accusation. This is important because revocation, restriction, and access renewal are socially meaningful changes in interpersonal access, not only technical actions. In the current interface, changes are phrased in descriptive terms such as a photo no longer being visible, access having changed, or a request having been accepted or rejected. This principle applies consistently across revoked-photo placeholders, state labels, and request-based feedback, ensuring that permission changes remain visible and understandable while minimising avoidable interpersonal friction.

\subsection{Consistency, and Honest Communication of Limits}

To achieve \textbf{NFR8}, the prototype is specified as operating alongside familiar WhatsApp-style secure messaging assumption. The design does not propose any change to the underlying end-to-end encryption model and does not attempt to solve revocation through cryptographic redesign. The specified permission management is framed as an additional interface and state-management layer that governs how photos remain visible or restricted within the platform context. This preserves conceptual continuity with users' expectations of secure WhatsApp-like communication while keeping the dissertation focused on interaction and consent management.

To achieve \textbf{NFR9}, the prototype communicates the limits of revocation explicitly and avoids implying complete erasure after disclosure. In the current interface, this is expressed through pre-send warning text in the composer, explanatory notes beneath shared photos, revoked-state placeholders, and co-subject request flows by stating that screenshots, saved copies, offline retention, and prior viewing cannot be fully undone. The design is intentionally scoped as platform-level visibility control rather than a guarantee of universal deletion. This means, the prototype remains honest about the partial and prospective nature of revocation and aims to calibrate user expectations about what its controls can and cannot enforce after a photo has been shared.

\section{Alternative Design Approaches}
One alternative design approach considered was a conversation-level privacy mode instead of having photo-level permissions. This approach would allow an entire chat to enter a privacy mode in which shared images are blurred by default and restrictions on permission apply across the whole chat.

However, this approach was not selected as the main approach. First, conversation-level controls were too broad for the project's central aim of exploring ongoing, revisable consent for photos. Different images within the same chat may require different permissions and durations and a single conversation-level mode could not represent these distinctions effectively. Second concern was about friction in everyday use. A conversation-level privacy mode would require users to think about and manage privacy at the chat level even when only some images are sensitive, which increases the mode confusion and interrupts the ordinary messaging flow. Lee et al. note that dynamic consent systems can face barriers such as consent fatigue and implementation complexity, and a chat-wide privacy mode risked increasing this burden in everyday messaging \cite{lee_identifying_2023}. For both of these reasons, such design carried a greater risk of being ignored by users over time.

Also at an earlier stage, a Figma-only prototype was considered but dropped because a coded version could represent live permission states, giving a closer approximation of the intended experience. The coded implementation is described in Chapter 5.
