\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Brief}
This chapter discusses the thesis as a whole, bringing together the design, implementation, and evaluation of Smart Photos to reflect on what the project shows about consent revocation in private messaging. It considers the implications of the findings, the limitations of the work, and directions for future research.

\section{Implications and Recommendations for Stakeholders}

This section outlines what the report suggests for four stakeholder groups.

\subsection*{Users}

The evaluation showed that participants who held realistic expectations about digital persistence were more likely to trust the revocation feature than those who expected complete erasure (Theme~2). This suggests that users benefit from understanding revocable permissions as meaningful but bounded. Such permissions serve to withdraw future platform-mediated access while not guaranteeing that all copies have been removed. Platforms that adopt similar features should therefore invest in clear, upfront communication of limits so that users can make informed sharing decisions. Its effectiveness also relied on the prototype design and how the features were presented to the participants.

\subsection*{Platform Designers}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Progressive disclosure for permissions.} The strongest usability concern (Theme~5) was that always-visible permission labels consumed excessive chat space. Designers should consider contextual menus or solutions like collapsible panels that keep permission information accessible without dominating the conversation view.
    \item \textbf{Neutral, system-mediated revocation language.} The evaluation found that neutral wording was perceived as non-accusatory by the majority of participants (Theme~3). This validates the use of descriptive, system-like phrasing for revocation notices and should be adopted as a design guideline for any post-send permission change.
    \item \textbf{Familiar terminology for co-subject features.} The term ``co-subject'' was universally misunderstood at first encounter and four participants independently compared the concept to Instagram tagging (Theme~4). Designers implementing multi-party photo permissions should use language that maps onto existing user mental models without introducing unfamiliar terminology.
    \item \textbf{Permission propagation across forwarding.} The Smart Photos model's approach of linking forwarded copies to the original permission state was understood by participants and maintained sender control across redistribution. This principle is relevant to how real platforms handle permission inheritance for forwarded media.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Platform Providers}

The evaluation findings demonstrate clear user demand for post and pre-send control that goes beyond the current delete-for-everyone model. WhatsApp's recent Advanced Chat Privacy feature moves in this direction but operates at the chat level rather than the photo level \cite{whatsapp_advanced_chat_privacy}. The Smart Photos model shows what photo-level permission management could look like in practice and the evaluation confirms that users both understood and valued this granularity. Providers and developers looking for an adoption of a similar feature should consider the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Honest Communication of Feature Scope:} The Theme~2 finding showed that some users dismissed controls they perceived as incomplete. New permission features should be accompanied by honest explanations of their scope to maintain user trust.


    \item \textbf{Permission Metadata Infrastructure:} The Smart Photos model requires each photo to carry a permission state. In production this means synchronising per-photo state across devices in real time, introducing storage, network, and computational overhead that must be planned for at scale.

    \item \textbf{Encryption Compatibility:} As discussed in specifications, permission metadata would need to travel through the 
    same encrypted channel as message content to avoid weakening the 
    privacy guarantees users expect.

    \item \textbf{Energy and Sustainability:} A deployed system 
    performing real-time permission lookups and cross-device 
    synchronisation at scale would carry a measurable energy cost 
    that providers should factor into their sustainability planning.
\end{itemize} 


\subsection*{Policymakers and Regulators}

The GDPR establishes a right to withdraw consent, and the UK Online Safety Act creates obligations for platforms to reduce harm from shared content. However, the evaluation shows that technical compliance alone does not satisfy user expectations. Delete-for-everyone, for instance, operates only prospectively (Section 2.5.2) \cite{politou_2018}. Two policy-relevant findings emerge. First, the partial and prospective nature of revocation (Theme~2, Design Principle~2) implies that regulatory standards should set realistic expectations for what ``withdrawal of consent'' can mean in messaging contexts. Second, the co-subject findings (Theme~4) suggest that multi-party consent in photo sharing is a policy area that current regulation has not caught up with.  Depicted individuals are widely regarded as having a legitimate stake in how shared photos are managed, yet no existing regulatory framework grants them a formal mechanism to request removal from content shared in private messaging.

\section{Prototype Scope and Limitations }
\label{sec:production-limitations}
The prototype fulfils the functional and non-functional specifications set out in Chapter~4 and the requirements have been implemented and verified through both manual checks and an automated test suite. The second implementation round addressed the interface and structural issues identified during the evaluation, while preserving the core permission model unchanged from the specification.

\textbf{Prototype scope.} Because the prototype operated as a front-end simulation without networking, persistent storage, or real encryption (Section~\ref{sec:tech-choices}), the evaluation findings apply to the interaction design and permission model rather than to production-level concerns. Participants interacted with a controlled, immediate simulation, meaning their responses may not fully reflect the experience of using revocable permissions under real network conditions where delays or failures could affect trust and perceived reliability. In a networked system, the canonical permission record would need to be stored server-side and queried by every client that holds a copy, raising questions around latency, offline access, and conflict resolution. A recipient viewing a forwarded photo while temporarily disconnected might see a stale permission state, and the system would need to decide whether to block rendering until the latest state is fetched. Platforms with end-to-end encryption such as WhatsApp would face the additional constraint that permission metadata must travel through the same encrypted channel as message content, since storing permissions in plaintext on the server would undermine the privacy guarantees that users expect. The evaluation also confirmed that structural limits shaped some participants' willingness to trust the feature (Theme 2), suggesting that user perceptions of control may shift in deployments that incorporate additional countermeasures.


\textbf{Co-subject scalability.} The prototype assumes that the sender manually flags a photo as depicting another person and that the co-subject is already a contact within the same platform. In practice, automated co-subject detection would require on-device face recognition or similar techniques, raising both accuracy and consent concerns. A false positive could notify someone who is not actually depicted, while a false negative could leave a genuine co-subject uninformed. If the co-subject is not a user of the platform, there is no channel through which to deliver the notification. These edge cases would need careful design work before the feature could be offered at scale.

Despite these limitations, the underlying principles of dynamic consent, multi-party awareness, expectation management, and neutral communication of access changes are not specific to WhatsApp and could inform the design of revocable sharing features on other platforms where users exchange personal media.

\section{Future Directions} \label{sec:future-directions}

The limitations and evaluation findings identified above point to several directions for future research, each grounded in a specific gap that the current project could not address.

\textbf{Live-prototype evaluation.} The present study used screenshot-based interviews, which captured participants' interpretive reasoning but not their real-time interaction behaviour. A follow-up study in which participants use the running prototype to engage in conversations would strengthen ecological validity and reveal usability issues tied to timing and sequential decision-making that static materials cannot capture.

\textbf{Broader and more diverse samples.} The present sample (N = 9, 8 male, 1 female, all university students aged 20–24) was deliberately narrow in service of information power (6.2.2), but the social-meaning findings in Theme 3 and the co-subject governance findings in Theme 4 are exactly the themes where demographic composition could plausibly matter. Follow-up work should recruit a gender-balanced sample and, separately, non-student populations, to test whether the responses documented here redistribute across demographic lines or hold across them.   

\textbf{Co-subject identification and governance.} The evaluation surfaced an unresolved tension between manual tagging, which depends on sender willingness, and automated detection, which introduces accuracy and privacy risks. Future work could explore hybrid approaches in which lightweight detection prompts the sender to confirm co-subject presence, reducing friction while preserving sender agency. The governance question about whether the co-subjects should hold equal, superior, or veto-level control also warrants dedicated experimental comparison.

\textbf{Integration with real platform infrastructure.} The production limitations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:production-limitations} raises several open engineering questions that a front-end simulation cannot resolve. Future work could address these by embedding the permission model within a real messaging architecture, beginning with a server-side permission store that clients query on each render, and measuring how revocation latency, offline caching strategies, and encrypted metadata distribution affect the reliability and trustworthiness of the feature under real network conditions.

\textbf{Longitudinal adoption.} The evaluation captured first-exposure reactions. Diary studies or longitudinal field trials tracking how users engage with revocable permissions over time would address whether the features retain their perceived value as routine settles, or whether users simplify their behaviour by defaulting to a small subset of permission configurations.
