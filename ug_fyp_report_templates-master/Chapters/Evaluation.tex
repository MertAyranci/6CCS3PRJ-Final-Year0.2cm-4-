\section{Interview-based Evaluation}

\subsection{Methodological Approach}

A qualitative interview-based evaluation was conducted using semi-structured interviews. This method was appropriate because the research aimed not only to gather feedback on the prototype, but also to investigate how participants made sense of new privacy controls and how they felt about the balance between stakeholders. Weiss argues that qualitative interviewing is useful when researchers need more than standardised answers, because it allows the interviewer to obtain participants' fuller accounts through explanation and discussion, and to access "perceptions and reactions" that are only available through their own accounts \cite{weiss_1994}. The semi-structured format supported this by combining a consistent scenario sequence across all participants with enough flexibility to probe misunderstandings, emotional reactions, and unanticipated concerns as they arose \cite{weiss_1994}. Follow-up probes were used selectively depending on each participant's responses, consistent with Weiss's view that the interviewer should direct discussion toward topics that matter to the study while judging when a response requires elaboration \cite{weiss_1994}.

\subsection{Participants and Sampling}
The selection of participants were chosen using purposive sampling. This approach was appropriate because the study's goal was not to produce a statistically representative account of the general population. Instead, it seeks feedback from people whose daily experiences qualified them to evaluate a prototype for messaging-based photo sharing. Weiss argues that, when a study is concerned with the experiences of people who share a common characteristic, the researcher needs a sample of people who together can represent the population of concern, and that with small qualitative samples it is often preferable to select respondents purposively in order to capture important variation rather than rely on probability sampling \cite{weiss_1994}.

The study specifically targeted adults aged 18-24, as this group is especially relevant to the context of the prototype and to contemporary messaging-based photo-sharing practices. 
In the UK, 18-24-year-olds remained the most active online in 2025, while 16-24-year-olds used the widest range of online communication platforms of any age group, indicating particularly high engagement with digital communication services \cite{OfcomOnlineNation2025,OfcomAdultsMediaUse2025}. This is directly relevant to the current study because online communication platforms include services used for messaging and calling, and WhatsApp has been identified as both the most popular app for those activities and the highest-reaching messaging and calls service across age groups \cite{OfcomOnlineNation2024,OfcomAdultsMediaUse2025}. Although some usage data is reported for the broader 16-24 category, the present study focuses on the adult subset of that group for practical and ethical reasons.

Nine participants were recruited in total (8 male, 1 female), all of whom were university students aged between 20 and 23. The sample was not gender-balanced, which is acknowledged as a limitation. However, the study did not aim to compare responses across demographic groups; its focus was on how participants interpreted and evaluated a specific interface design. In qualitative research of this kind, the analytical priority is depth of individual accounts rather than demographic representativeness \cite{braun2019, malterud_2016}. Malterud, Siersma and Guassora's concept of information power further supports this position: when the study aim is narrow and the sample is specific to the phenomenon under investigation, fewer and less diverse participants can still yield sufficient analytical insight \cite{malterud_2016}. Gender was recorded descriptively to contextualise the sample but was not used as an analytic category. All participants reported regular use of WhatsApp or similar messaging platforms, with a minimum of six years of experience, ensuring familiarity with the photo-sharing conventions and interface patterns that the prototype builds upon.

A target of 8-10 participants was set for this study. This number was considered appropriate for a focused qualitative evaluation with a relatively specific participant group and a narrow research aim. Rather than seeking statistical outcomes, the study aimed to identify the recurring patterns in how participants understood the prototype. This is supported by methodological literature suggesting that smaller interview samples can be sufficient in focused studies. Hennink, Kaiser and Marconi found that code saturation was reached at 9 interviews, while Guest, Bunce and Johnson found that thematic saturation occured within the first twelwe interviws, with basic elements of higher-level themes were already present by six interviews \cite{Hennink_2017,Guest_Bunce_Johnson_2006}. In this case, data saturation was reached by the ninth participant, suggesting that the sample was sufficient to generate meaningful evaluative insight within the scope of the dissertation.


\subsection{Inrerview Procedure}

Each interview will be conducted individually using two main materials: a written interview guide and a set of screenshots taken from the prototype. The interview guide structures the session into a consent stage, a warm-up section, five scenarios,and a final wrap-up. This use of a  guide is consistent with Weiss’s (1994) view that an interview guide should function as a prompt and checklist for the interviewer rather than as a script that is followed mechanically \cite{weiss_1994}. The visual materials consist of fictional example screenshots illustrating the main design states. 

At the start of each session, participants will be read a standardised consent statement explaining the purpose of the study, the nature of the prototype evaluation, and the fact that the participant is not being tested. They will also be informed that they may skip any question, stop the interview at any time, and choose whether or not to be audio-recorded. This procedure is consistent with DeJonckheere and Vaughn’s (2019) recommendation that participants should be informed when audio recording is used for data collection and that they should be able to refuse recording if they prefer. If consent is given, the interview will be audio-recorded so that responses can later be transcribed and analysed using the participant’s exact wording. Verbatim transcription is important because it preserves participants’ exact language for analysis \cite{dejonckheere_2019}.

The interview begins with a short warm-up about participants’ current photo-sharing behaviour on  WhatsApp. This order follows DeJonckheere and Vaughn’s (2019) guidance that interviews should begin with easy, context-setting questions before moving into deeper discussion \cite{dejonckheere_2019}. Participants are then shown a series of prototype scenarios in a fixed order using screenshots. These screenshots are based on the set scenarios which includes pre-send permissions screen, the post-send permission-change screen, the recipient-side view after access has changed, the forwarding/request-access-again flow, and co-subject related flow. For each screen participants asked to describe what they think happening. This is intentional, because participants’ first interpretations are part of the evaluative data, especially in relation to understandability. This reflects DeJonckheere and Vaughn’s (2019) recommendation that interview questions should be open-ended, with follow-up questions used to explore responses in more detail \cite{dejonckheere_2019}. Probes are used selectively rather than mechanically, so the interview remains conversational. This approach is consistent with Weiss (1994), who argues that rigidly covering every point in an interview guide can reduce the quality of qualitative data \cite{weiss_1994}. The interviews use fictional example photos rather than participants’ own personal images. This keeps the scenarios realistic while avoiding unnecessary disclosure of private content, which is appropriate given the potentially sensitive nature of the topic \cite{dejonckheere_2019}. At the end of the interview, participants are asked a short set of reflection questions about whether the design would increase their sense of control, affect their trust in the platform, and which elements felt most clear, useful, confusing, or uncomfortable. They are also invited to add anything else that stood out.

\subsection{Data Analysis Method}

Interview recordings were transcribed and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis \cite{braun2006, braun2019}. This approach was appropriate because the evaluation aimed to examine how participants interpreted and judged the prototype, rather than simply count preferences or identify isolated usability issues. Reflexive thematic analysis treats the researcher as an active participant in knowledge production rather than as a neutral coder, which suited the interpretive aims of this study \cite{braun2019}.

The analysis used an inductive coding strategy. Rather than applying a pre-defined codebook, codes were developed directly from the data through repeated engagement with the transcripts. Quotes capturing relevant perceptions, reactions, and experiences were first extracted from each transcript, then organised into categories based on recurring patterns across participants. Code names were generated to label these categories, and each quote was matched to the relevant codes. Through iterative review and refinement, the codes were then grouped into five broader themes. This bottom-up approach is consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, in which codes and themes are understood as actively constructed by the researcher through sustained engagement with the dataset rather than pre-existing in the data awaiting discovery \cite{braun2006, braun2019}.

The analytic process followed a structured sequence from raw data to themes Figure~\ref{fig:miro-overview}.

Transcripts were first read repeatedly to support familiarisation with the dataset as a whole. Key quotes capturing relevant perceptions, reactions, and experiences were then extracted and placed onto a Miro board as colour-coded sticky notes, with each colour representing the interview section from which the quote originated (warm-up, scenarios 1 through 5, and final reflections). This visual arrangement supported comparison across participants and helped codes and excerpts to be grouped spatially as patterns became more apparent. This process producedapproximately 500 quotes across all nine participants, which were further refined to around 250 analytically relevant quotes.

An initial codebook of 56 fine-grained codes was developed through the inductive process described above. These codes were informed in part by concepts identified in the literature review and research questions, while also being revised and expanded in response to recurring patterns in the interview data. To support efficient and consistent coding across the dataset, the 56 codes were consolidated into 20 working codes grouped into seven conceptual categories: Control and Agency, Understandability and Clarity, Trust and Transparency, Social and Emotional Impact, Forwarding and Redistribution, Co-Subject and Multi-Party Privacy, and UI and Design Feedback. Both the full and simplified codebooks are included in Appendix~\ref{app:codebook}.

Coding was conducted primarily at the semantic level, focusing on participants' explicit interpretations and stated reactions, while also attending to broader patterns of meaning where relevant. Each quote was tagged with the relevant code identifiers from the simplified codebook. Codes were then reviewed and grouped into broader candidate themes based on recurring patterns in how participants understood the prototype and evaluated its implications. Candidate themes were checked against the coded data and the wider dataset to ensure they captured meaningful patterns across multiple participants rather than reflecting isolated responses. Themes were refined iteratively until they provided a coherent and internally consistent structure for presenting the evaluation findings. This is consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, which does not treat coding reliability or consensus between coders as markers of quality, but instead positions researcher subjectivity and reflexive engagement with the data as central to the analytic process \cite{braun2019}.

\section{Thematic Analysis Findings}
Section analyses and discusses the findings of the thematic analysis in detail.

\subsection{Theme 1: From Binary to Revocable Consent}
The first theme captures how participants percieved current WhatsApp photo sharing controls as inadequate and how the prototype's revisable permissions model strengtens the consent revocation. Participants consistently framed current WhatsApp photo sharing as too binary, typically reduced to either permenant availability or view-once, with no support for changing permissions later. The prototype's ability ot configure permissions before and after sending was recognised by all nine participants as an improvement and frequently connected to personal experiences. P1 described this need in explicitly relational terms: \textit{``when I send some more private pictures to my close friends or my closest relationships... when my status of my relationship changes, I want to go back and undo what I send''} (P1, Warm-up). Furthermore P5 connected this to the risk of accidental disclosure: \textit{``sometimes I can accidentally send pictures to the wrong people and it could have major consequences''} (P5, Warm-up). The shared underlying concern was not simply about deleting content, but about maintaining ongoing agency over shared material as the context evolves.

Several participants explicitly criticised the limited range of existing controls. As P1 put it, \textit{``we have two options... no middle option because the two of them are both extremes''} (P1, Warm-up), while P4 summarised the broader issue by stating that \textit{``in most apps, once the photo is sent, you do not really have much control anymore''} (P4, Warm-up). The prototype addressed this gap by allowing permissions to be configured at the point of sending and revised later.

For some participants, the increased sense of control extended beyond personal comfort to behavioural change. P5 suggested the feature would expand their use of the platform: \textit{``This would definitely increase my use of this platform for photos. It could also make me consider using it more for business, maybe contracts''} (P5, Scenario 2). P2 similarly framed the co-subjectfeature in precautionary and regulatory terms, suggesting it could be useful in legal contexts because it provided a mechanism for showing that permission-related steps had been taken and for reducing future disputes over shared personal data. This indicates that revisable permissions may not only reduce anxiety for existing sharing behaviour but also enable new categories of photo sharing that users currently avoid.
 
These findings are consistent with the literature on dynamic and ongoing consent reviewed in Chapter2, which argues that one-off authorisation models are poorly suited to interpersonal messaging because the meaning of permission can shift after disclosure \cite{im_yes_2021, kaye_dynamic_2015, wilbanks_design_2018}.    

\subsection{Theme 2: Bounded Honesty and System Limitations}
This theme evaluates whether the prototype achieved the design goal set out in Section 4.2.2: communicating revocation as limited but meaningful control rather than implying complete erasure. In practice, the results only partially supported this expectation. For some participants, the screenshot warning and related explanations made the system feel more realistic and therefore more trustworthy, for others, the same honesty weakened the feature’s perceived value by foregrounding how little control can be guaranteed after disclosure.


The most positive framing came from P4, who described the warning as making the design feel more credible: \textit{``That may make the feature feel less powerful, but it also makes it more trustworthy and realistic''} (P4, Scenario 1). P7 similarly treated the disclaimer as a useful prompt for reflection before sending, although both P7 and P9 felt, it needed greater visual prominence to function effectively. This shows that the prototype generally succeeded in managing expectations for participants who had previously accepted revocation as prospective and partial, but that this was strongly dependent on the warning being properly placed and clearly visible.
 
At the other end of the spectrum, P3 interpreted the same information as undermining the feature's purpose: \textit{``it does make you feel honest, but then at the same time it makes you think, what's the point of sending it using these revocable permissions in the first place?''} (P3, Scenario 1). This scepticism extended to P3's overall willingness to use the feature for sensitive content. P9 did not notice the warning at all until it was pointed out: \textit{``... the disclaimer is good but I didn't see it before until you pointed it out. So I think it'll be better if it was bigger''} (P9, Scenario 1).
 
The split in responses maps onto a tension identified in the design rationale (Section 4.2.2), where expectation management was positioned as a core design responsibility. The findings suggest that this principle is endorsed by participants who already hold a realistic mental model of digital persistence, but may discourage those who expect stronger guarantees. This is consistent with Schnitzler et al.'s observation that users do not always accurately understand what deletion or revocation actions achieve, and that clearer interface explanations can improve comprehension \cite{schnitzler_2020}. The practical implication is that the warning's value depends not only on its content but on its visual prominence and framing. Several participants suggested it should be larger, more prominently placed, or accompanied by icons to ensure it is noticed before sending.
 
Platform trust responses further illuminate this theme. Participants who accepted the bounded nature of control tended to report increased trust in the platform (P1, P2, P4, P6, P8), reasoning that the feature demonstrated the platform's commitment to user privacy. P1 stated: \textit{``it would make me trust the platform more, because it genuinely shows they want me to have control over my own privacy, which is something you cannot see everywhere in today's world''} (P1, Final Reflections). In contrast, participants who questioned the feature's effectiveness reported no change in platform trust (P3, P5, P7, P9) or expressed broader scepticism about platform intentions (P9): \textit{``it wouldn't change my trust in the platform, because most apps take sensitive information. I still don't trust them completely''} (P9, Final Reflections). P3 reframed the issue as interpersonal rather than institutional: \textit{``the platform, no difference at all. It just makes a difference to the senders and how they interact''} (P3, Final Reflections).
 
Beyond the screenshot warning itself, five participants identified a deeper technological limitation that the prototype cannot address. Because content is ultimately displayed on a physical screen, it can always be captured using a separate device. As P7 put it, \textit{the photo is still displayed on the device. So you can always take another device and take a picture of the screen"} (P7, Warm-up). Some participants also expected the design to push further against these limits through available countermeasures. P5 suggested that screenshots could render as a black screen, reporting having seen this implemented elsewhere (P5, Scenario 4). These observations are consistent with Heath et al.'s forensic analysis of ephemeral messaging, which documents multiple preservation routes including forwarding, screenshotting, and photographing content before it disappears \cite{heath_2023}.


\subsection{Theme 3: Revocation as a Social Act}
Participants did not treat revocation as a neutral technical action. Instead, they seen it as a socially meaningful act that could signal trust-mistrust, changing intentions or shifts in the relationship. The feedbacks ranged from acceptance to offence, with majority of participants suggesting that the social meaning of revocation depended on context, specifically the type of photo and the relationship involved.

Several participants expressed their acceptance by reasoning through reciprocity. So they stated that if they would want the same power as a sender, they should accept it as a recipient. P7 articulated this most clearly:\textit{``there's no harsh feelings,... I'm totally happy with how it works... you don't play the role of just sender or just recipient. When you're using an app like this, you're actively being both''} (P7, Scenario 3). 

However, other participants attached more negative meanings to the same action. P2 anticipated feeling \textit{``a bit suspicious''} and \textit{``annoyed''} (P2, Scenario 3), while P5 associated revocation with possible mistrust. The strongest reaction came from P8, who said: \textit{``I would feel offended, like,... at first you sent this and what happened that made you erase this photo?''} (P8, Scenario 3). These responses suggest that even when the interface uses neutral wording, revocation still carries an interpersonal signal that cannot be fully neutralised through design alone.

The request-access feature was designed to balane sender control with recipient-side agency. Again the responses were mixed. P5 reimagined the feature as a timed-access mechanism through a concrete scenario: \textit{``one guy buys all the tickets... he could revoke access and then when friends are at the entrance, they could request access''} (P5, Scenario 3). However, P8 questioned the logic of requesting back something that was deliberately withdrawn: \textit{``it makes things more weird. He revoked it for a reason. Why should I want it again?''} (P8, Scenario 3). P9 raised a practical concern about abuse: \textit{``if the person keeps spamming the request access option, it might be bothersome. So a timer, so they can't request as frequently''} (P9, Scenario 3).

These findings connect to several theoretical perspectives from Chapter 2. The range of emotional reactions is consistent with CPM theory's concept of boundary turbulence, where changes to previously established privacy rules can produce conflict or discomfort \cite{petronio_cpm_2020}. The social meaning participants attached to revocation also supports the design rationale's third principle (Section 4.2.3), which aimed to minimise social friction through neutral wording (a goal that the wording succesfully achieved, as 88\% of the participants described it as neutral), but which does not completely eliminate the relational signal that exists there in the revocation process itself.

\subsection{Theme 4: Co-Subject Privacy as a Multi-Consent Problem}
This theme captures the participants' reactions to the co-subject feature which is the most complex and contradictory set of responses in the evaluation. This feature was simultaneously the most confusing and for several participants the most important feature in the prototype. At first each participant (9/9) couldn't understand the 'co-subject in photo' label and required explanation before they could engage with the concept. However, after the explanation, majority recognised the value of protecting the privacy of depicted individuals.

The terminological confusion was universal and immediate. P1 asked directly: \textit{``co-subject in photo. Can you please explain what is co-subject in photo?''} (P1, Scenario 1). P9 attempted to interpret it through an existing mental model: \textit{``the co-subject is a bit hard to understand, but I think it's like how tagging works on Instagram''} (P9, Scenario 1). The tagging comparison was made independently by four participants (P1, P8, P9, and implicitly P5), suggesting that tagging is the closest available reference point for users encountering multi-party photo permissions for the first time.

Furthermore, participants generally accepted the privacy concern the feature was trying to address. P3 expressed this most strongly: \textit{``even if you're not the one who took the picture, if you're a subject, you should have the rights as to whether or not other people should have access''} (P3, Scenario 5). P9 similarly linked the feature to protection against harm, arguing that equal power between sender and co-subject would be preferable in cases where the depicted person might otherwise be exposed without consent. These responses suggest that participants saw depicted individuals as having a legitimate stake in downstream photo sharing, even when they were not the original sender.

At the same time, participants raised substantial concerns about feasibility. P8 questioned the fundamental premise: \textit{``why would the sender at the first point bother to tag the co-subject?''} (P8, Scenario 5), highlighting that the feature depends entirely on voluntary sender action. P2 suggested automated identification as an alternative, but P5 immediately identified the accuracy risk: \textit{``you could be on the street and another guy looked at the camera and if the app detects that as a co-subject...''} (P5, Scenario 5). This tension between manual tagging (which depends on sender willingness) and automated detection (which introduces accuracy and privacy risks) reflects a challenge also identified by Xu et al., who propose privacy-preserving facial recognition for co-owner detection but acknowledge the dependency on reliable identification \cite{xu_my_2017}. P1 identified a further privacy tension where notifying a co-subject could reveal that the sender had shared their image in a private conversation, potentially harming the privacy of that conversation itself. To reduce friction, P5 proposed persistent consent preferences: \textit{``always accept from this person, or never accept''} (P5, Scenario 5) which would function as contact-level trust settings, reducing per-photo overhead. Together, these concerns highlights while the co-subject concept is valued in principle, its implementation must navigate a trade-off between identification accuracy, user friction, and sender willingness to participate.

On the question of who should hold the strongest control, opinions differed into three different clusters. The majority (P1, P2, P4, P5, P9) favoured equal or balanced control between the sender and co-subject. P7 and P8 favoured giving the co-subject stronger control, with P7 arguing: \textit{``I'd favour the co-subject to have the upper hand because they might not even been asked for permission. The sender might just be sending it to random people''} (P7, Scenario 5). A third group (P3, P5, P6) advocated a veto model in which any single objection would prevent sharing: P6 stated that \textit{``if there's 5 or 6 people and only one says no, then it can't be sent...''} (P6, Scenario 5).

These findings are consistent with the multi-party privacy conflict literature, which argues that co-owned content creates situations where the person sharing is not the only person exposed to privacy consequences \cite{cherubini_2021}. The divergence in participants’ governance preferences reflects a broader problem identified in the literature where co-owned photos cannot be governed adequately through sender-only control. Existing approaches offer partial support for multi-party privacy, but still leave unresolved how conflicting stakeholder interests should be managed once an image begins to circulate \cite{cherubini_2021, xu_my_2017, zhang_go-sharing_2023}. Therefore, the prototype addresses this by giving the depicted person a role after disclosure through a co-subject revocation request, rather than leaving control entirely with the sender. Overall, the findings suggest that co-subject control was conceptually valued, but would require clearer terminology and more carefully engineered governance before it could function in practice.


\subsection{Theme 5: Interface Legibility and Presentation Efficiency}
This theme concerns how permission information was visually presented in the chat interface and how participants evaluated the trade-off between visibility and usability. Unlike earlier themes, which focused on the meaning and social implications of revocable sharing, this theme focuses specifically on interface density, readability, and the practical burden of displaying permissions inline within everyday messaging.

The most consistent concern was the space occupied by permission labels beneath photos. P6 noted: \textit{``imagine there are quite a few photos and it's this long list with all these features. It can be annoying to scroll''} (P6, Scenario 2). P7 raised an accessibility dimension: \textit{``it's quite wordy... it might take a while to process for a user with accessibility issues''} (P7, Scenario 3). P8 described the same issue from the sender's perspective, observing that sending multiple photos with individual permission displays would make the chat difficult to navigate (P8, Scenario 2). Participants suggested several alternatives: P6 proposed a long-press contextual menu following existing WhatsApp patterns, P5 suggested on/off toggle sliders instead of text-based labels, and P1 preferred permissions hidden behind a tap rather than displayed inline. These suggestions converge on a shared preference which indicates permission information should be accessible but not persistently visible.

The concerns are consistent with established usability guidance. Nielsen’s heuristics emphasise that interfaces should avoid displaying information that is irrelevant or rarely needed, since every additional unit of information competes with more important content and reduces its visibility \cite{nng_heuristics}. The same guidance also notes that users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing, which is relevant to participants’ confusion over the distinction between view-once and time-limit controls \cite{nng_heuristics}.

Two further clarity issues also emerged. P8 and P9 both perceived view-once and time-limit as overlapping features, with P9 stating: \textit{``viewing it once and putting a time limit, I think they are similar. Both accomplishes the same thing''} (P9, Scenario 1). This suggests the distinction between these two controls needs clearer differentiation in the interface. P8 also noted inconsistency in how revocation was communicated, observing that the revoked state used both passive and active voice and \textit{``basically repeats it three times in different places''} (P8, Scenario 3).

At the same time, majority of the participants recognised that the prototype's visual consistency with WhatsApp's existing patterns supported learnability. P5 noted: \textit{``it's integrated to the WhatsApp system and the WhatsApp user interface, it looks like I'm already familiar with this stuff''} (P5, Final Reflections). This suggests that the design principle of consistency with existing interaction patterns (Section 4.2.5) was effective, and that the challenge is not the permission model itself but the density of its always-visible presentation within the chat.

\section {Summary of Findings}
Taken together, the five themes indicate that the prototype's core permission model was well understood and valued as increasing user control for shared photos compared to WhatsApp's current model. This positive overall assessment was also reflected in participants' clarity ratings, which ranged from 3 to 5 with a median of 4. However, responses also showed that this control was understood as socially sensitive and dependent on clear presentation. Participants appreciated the ability to revise permissions after sending, particularly in situations involving sensitive content, accidental disclosure, or changing relationships, but they also recognised that revocation could not fully prevent screenshots or external capture. In addition, revocation was interpreted not only as a technical control but as a socially meaningful act, while the co-subject feature highlighted both the importance and complexity of multi-party consent. Several issues identified across the themes were addressed in the second implementation phase, especially those concerning interface clarity and the communication of revocation. Taken together, these findings suggest that revocable photo sharing was well received in principle, but would require careful balancing of agency, realism, social acceptability, and interface simplicity in practice.
